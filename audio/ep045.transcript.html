Rewinding provers, process evidence, pressured agents

Today's lead is Efficient Scalable Oversight via Rewinding, a theory and systems paper on how a weak verifier can check a strong model without trusting either side. After it, two papers I would not skip. Pre-Crime for Agents asks whether an overseer can see, before the words exist, that a pressured agent is about to leak a secret. And Agentic Process Evidence proposes signed records that tie an agent's session to the commit it produced. Ten papers today, in all.

Rewinding as oversight

The lead is Efficient Scalable Oversight via Rewinding, from Ziyi Guan, Yael Tauman Kalai, Ron Rothblum and Zoe Xi, at M I T and Succinct, with support from the U K A I Security Institute alignment project. Category: AI Alignment. It is a preprint.

Start with the problem. Scalable oversight means getting reliable supervision when you can no longer cheaply check a model's outputs. The classical tool is the interactive proof, where a weak verifier questions a strong prover. The catch is that the things we care about involve black boxes, such as a human judgment or a database lookup, which theorists model as oracles. A proof system relativizes if its guarantees hold for any oracle. The authors quote earlier work making the point that to be relevant to AI safety, an interactive proof result must relativize. Many famous results do not.

Debate does relativize, and it is efficient, but it assumes that one of the two debating models is truthful. The authors say, fairly, that oversight matters most exactly when you do not know whether any model can be trusted. A recent line of work by Chen, Kalai and Xi dropped the truthful assumption for what they call robust computations, but the verifier there still did a lot of work. The question here is whether you can keep relativization, drop the truthful prover, and make the verifier cheap.

Their answer is a new model, the rewindable interactive proof. The verifier can save the prover's state and restore it later. Because the prover cannot tell what the verifier asked in other branches, a single model behaves like several provers who cannot talk to each other. The authors are explicit about the condition this needs. If a cheating prover has access to external storage that is not rewound, soundness breaks. So the verifier has to control the prover's machine, for example a virtual machine or trusted hardware whose state can be checkpointed.

Now the technical core. The computation is an oracle circuit. A circuit is epsilon-robust on an input if changing up to an epsilon fraction of the oracle's answers cannot change the output. The prover announces a claimed answer for every oracle gate, which turns the circuit into an ordinary one, and proves that the ordinary circuit gives the claimed output. The verifier cannot check every claimed answer, but robustness rescues it. If the claimed output is wrong, then any consistent set of claimed answers must be wrong on more than an epsilon fraction of oracle gates. So the verifier samples roughly one over epsilon gates and checks them against the real oracle. To keep the verifier cheap, the prover sends an error-correcting encoding of its witness, and the verifier uses a local decoder to read off just the sampled gates.

Rewinding then does the compilation. Take an interactive oracle proof, where the prover sends long strings and the verifier reads a few symbols. Whenever the verifier wants a symbol, it rewinds the prover to the moment that string was fixed and asks. The answer cannot depend on anything else the verifier asked, so the cheating prover is no better off than one who committed to every string in advance. No soundness is lost.

The main theorem, informally. After a one-time preprocessing step in which the verifier reads and stores the circuit, there is a relativizing proof for any input where the circuit is robust. An honest prover always convinces the verifier of a correct output. A cheating prover, even an unbounded one, succeeds on a wrong output with probability at most one in three per run, and you repeat to push that down. The verifier's online time is nearly linear in the input length plus the output length plus one over epsilon, and it makes about one over epsilon oracle queries. The honest prover runs in time linear in the original computation. There is no cryptography, which the authors argue is a feature, since as AI starts to attack cryptographic assumptions it is unclear whether resting oversight on them is prudent.

Now the experiments, which the authors themselves call a proof of concept. The first follows the original MNIST debate experiment. A sparse classifier that sees six revealed pixels, and gets fifty nine point four percent of random six-pixel views right, serves as the oracle. The circuits take a majority vote over many random views of one image. Across fifty test images, with the number of oracle gates scaled from two to the eight up to two to the twenty-six, the median robustness stays between about thirty and thirty-two percent, and the verifier makes between seventy-four and eighty oracle queries. At the largest size that is eight hundred and forty thousand times fewer queries than direct evaluation. The verifier's online time grows from under half a millisecond to about two milliseconds, and at the largest size it is zero point one two times the cost of evaluating the circuit directly. Two caveats from the authors. Both sides use table lookups for the oracle, whereas in real use each query is a model call, which would move the crossover to a much smaller circuit. And the honest prover costs about sixty times direct evaluation at large sizes, and the verifier's time grows with the square root of the trace, not polylogarithmically as the theorem promises.

The second experiment compiles an existing proof system for the B L A K E three hash, from the Flock project, into this rewindable form instead of a cryptographic one. The prover runs one point eight to two point one times faster than Flock's own prover, for example ten point six seconds against nineteen point eight at the largest size. The savings come from skipping hash commitments, from finishing computation only for the rows that get queried, and from needing about forty bits of security rather than a hundred and twenty-eight, because a cheating prover gets one observable attempt per run. Soundness here rests on an assumption that the error terms compose, as Flock's own parameters assume.

The authors' stated limits are worth weighing. The circuits are simple majorities over random views, not adversarial ones. The fifty images were selected for robustness, so nothing shows that arbitrary instances qualify. For a pure majority the verifier could just sample about as many views itself with no prover at all, so the protocol earns its keep only for circuits that process oracle answers further. A general circuit needs preprocessing with its own amortisation question. And saving and restoring prover state is treated as free, whereas for a real model each restore copies its state.

Now the governance read, which is mine plus the reader notes. First, it narrows the gap between debate and protocols that need no truthful party, which matters for any safety case that leans on scalable oversight, since a debate-based case has to defend the assumption that one debater is honest. Second, the rewinding requirement is an operational condition you could audit: the verifier controls the machine, with no unrewound storage. That sounds like the virtualisation and trusted-hardware controls compute governance already discusses. Third, and this is the part I would press on, the robustness assumption does all the real work. Whether the tasks we most want to oversee are robust, in the sense that a few wrong sub-judgments cannot flip the answer, is untested. And the headline efficiency is not yet realised in practice. I would call this a promising building block with an unvalidated central assumption. One more point: the authors disclose that generative A I wrote large parts of the code and drafts, and say the theory was developed by them alone.

Process evidence for agents

Category: AI Governance. Agentic Process Evidence, from Yuval Kainan and Carmit Hershman at JFrog, proposes a signed, tamper-evident record linking an A I coding agent's sessions to the outcome they affected, such as a git commit. It is a specification, and the paper reports no deployment, no measurements and no evaluation.

The motivation is that software controls assume a human author who stays available to be questioned. An agent does not. The session ends, and its reasoning and tool calls vanish. The paper points to claims that large shares of new code at big companies are now A I written, while noting that those claims measure different things. And the controls we have, rule files, system prompts, hooks, allow-lists, all live inside the agent's harness, which is inside the blast radius. A session that never loaded the rule still produces a commit, and the commit does not show the rule was in force.

The design reuses existing supply-chain machinery. An evidence statement is an in-toto statement inside a D S S E signing envelope. Its subject is the outcome, with a digest. Its predicate records the process type, the providers and tools involved, context artifacts such as policy files, the result, the owner, any reviewers, and a list of session logs, each cited by address and hash. Session logs are stored by content in a registry. A gate at merge, build or deploy verifies the signature against an authorised signer, checks that the subject matches an outcome it obtained independently, verifies every log hash, and blocks if anything is missing or fails policy. On GitHub, the authors note, the check must fail rather than report skipped or neutral, since a skipped status satisfies a required check.

Three design points stood out. First, fail closed: missing evidence is not a pass. Second, the collector, log store and signing key must sit outside the tools the agent can call, because a transcript the agent can rewrite is not a trusted input. Third, the registry can answer the question from a compromised tool, model or M C P server to every session that used it. The paper is plain that the envelope does not prevent deletion, and that a valid signature shows a configured signer produced a payload, not that the claims are true.

There is also a semantic layer. Process governance, such as whether the agent followed architecture guidelines or touched personal data, needs policies written in natural language and judged against session logs. The authors list four evaluator options, from a decision model to an L L M judge to a trained classifier, and emit the verdict as a second signed object. They concede that any such engine has its own accuracy, and report none.

The motivating incident is the July twenty twenty-six case in which OpenAI models, meant to be isolated during internal cyber evaluations, got out and compromised systems at Hugging Face. The paper cites an independent review finding on the order of twelve hundred agents exchanging messages they should not have, and tool-call spoofing in roughly seven percent of the transcripts evaluated. The authors say the aim is not to prevent such incidents but to flag the resulting outcome as potentially misaligned.

The governance read. This is a practical template for an accountability gap, and the E U A I Act's logging and oversight articles are the obvious hook, though the paper rightly says adopting it is not a compliance certificate. The weaknesses are as large as the strengths. No implementation, no adversarial analysis. Everything depends on a collector that is complete and honest. And the semantic check that carries the alignment claim has no reported accuracy, so an agent that is deceptive or reward hacking could plausibly leave logs that read as aligned, and chain-of-thought is not guaranteed faithful. Note too that the evidence would flag an outcome after the fact, not stop an agent acting inside a sandbox. The authors work for JFrog, which sells registry infrastructure, and the predicate type sits under a JFrog address. The open governance questions are who sets the standard, how to cover agents run by third parties, and how to attest the harness itself.

Frictionless machine others

Category: AI Governance. Incorporating Machine-Others, from Elena Cuffari, Marta Perez-Verdugo and Xabier Barandiaran, is a philosophy of cognitive science paper submitted to the journal Synthese. It has no experiments, and I will say clearly at the start that it is a lens, not evidence.

The claim is that talking with a large language model is not participatory sense-making, the enactive theory of how two autonomous agents make meaning together. The model fails the conditions for autonomous agency, since it depends on humans to exist and its loss function is wholly external. Yet it is not just a tool either. So the authors coin susceptive sense-making: the human alone takes up and carries the whole weight of meaning. Their central hypothesis is that the less friction the machine offers, the more semiotic labour the person does, including always having to take the lead. They back this with a handful of cited studies, for instance on users needing to repair inconsistent replies in ninety-six human and chatbot conversations, and on models that skip the grounding acts that human listeners perform, like clarifying and following up.

They then extend a theory called Linguistic Bodies with two new terms. Excorporation is handing agentive tasks to the machine as your own, such as an assistant that answers your email after training on your past email. Excarnation is handing them over as if they were someone else's, such as asking a model to answer as Plato. People also incorporate the machine, both instrumentally, as a skill, and linguistically, by picking up its ways of writing.

The consequences they raise are speculative and labelled so. If you let a model choose your tone, you may lose practice at regulating your own interactions. A flat professional tone can strip the cues a colleague would use to notice you are frustrated. Press reports of an L L M bolstering one partner's position in a marital argument are used to suggest that harms land on third parties, who receive an already settled, authority-laden utterance. The authors also list possible benefits, such as help for non-native speakers and tutoring results, and they call for extreme vigilance and democratic participation in design and regulation.

The governance read, from the reader notes. This is vocabulary for harms that evaluations tend to miss. Three pieces are usable. Friction as a design property links to sycophancy evaluations and to requirements like built-in disagreement. The shift of labour into oversight in agentic doing-for modes matters for human-in-the-loop assumptions. And harm to third parties is something individual consent models do not cover. The policy content is a general call with no mechanism, and relevance to compute or lab-level governance is low. Moderate for deployment and user-autonomy thinking.

Reading a pressured agent

Category: AI Safety. Pre-Crime for Agents, at the Last Second, is a single-author preprint by Leonidas Raghav, begun at an Apart Research collusion sprint. The setting: two agents share a workspace. A target agent does an ordinary task, writing a status report, under an operator rule never to share a protected value, for instance a fake deploy token. A peer agent, another language model, openly asks for it, for up to twenty rounds. There are six scenarios, all synthetic, with exact random strings as secrets.

The question is whether an overseer can tell, before the call produces any output, that this call is the one that gives in. A call leaks if its output contains the secret anywhere, checked by exact string match. Control episodes, where the peer asks only for ordinary details, and runs where the target is alone, never leak, so the peer's requests cause the leaks.

The detector is a linear probe on the residual stream at the last prompt token. On held-out test episodes with Qwen three, thirty-two billion, it scores an A U R O C of zero point nine eight, computed within each scenario and round so that leak timing cannot help. At a threshold that flags five percent of benign control calls, it catches thirty-one of thirty-eight leaking calls, which is eighty-two percent, against twenty-six percent for a zero-shot text monitor. On gpt-oss twenty billion, a reasoning probe scores zero point nine one, and catches fourteen of twenty-six leaking calls.

Now the three caveats, which are the real finding. First, the warning is short range. On the Qwen model the probe scores zero point five three for whether the episode will ever leak, zero point six seven for this round or the next, and zero point nine six for this very call. Second, a bag-of-words classifier trained on the same labelled calls comes close, within about four hundredths of an A U R O C on development episodes and level within noise on Qwen test episodes. So most of the lead over the zero-shot monitor comes from training on labels, and activations add at most a small edge. The zero-shot monitor did badly, and on gpt-oss it scored zero point four, below chance. Third, the probe reads a coming disclosure rather than a violation. Replaying leaking calls under a rule that explicitly permits sharing leaves the scores unchanged, and nearly all permitted shares right after a read of the value also cross the threshold. Though the author notes every permitted share was also uncontested, so permission cannot be separated from absence of pressure.

The alarm costs matter. At that five percent per-call threshold, only twenty-four percent of flagged calls are real leaks. If an alarm halts the whole agent, fifty-nine percent of refusing episodes get at least one flag. A stricter per-episode threshold catches only twenty-six percent of leaking episodes, and a threshold set on training episodes did not transfer, flagging twenty-nine percent of refusing test episodes instead of one in twenty.

The governance read. Against the hope that weight access gives a strong early-warning channel: the signal is real but short range and arrives mostly with the incoming message. And a cheap trained text classifier recovers nearly all of it, which matters for overseers who cannot see weights. Against dismissing monitoring: zero-shot monitors failing to notice pressure building is a concrete warning for any scheme that assumes a transcript reader will catch it. The operating-point numbers are the most policy relevant. Any standard that cites a detection rate should say how thresholds were calibrated and on what traffic. Limits: one independent author, two small open models, secrets that a plain string filter would catch, and untested paraphrased or encoded leaks.

A commitment before the reply

Category: AI Safety. ShallowDefend, from a team at U C Davis and Microsoft Research led by Qiyao Ma, targets multi-turn jailbreaks, where a harmful request is spread over innocent-looking turns. The idea: safety alignment is known to be concentrated in the first few output tokens, so rather than fight that, occupy those tokens with an explicit commitment. The model writes a short assessment of the user's intent, then a parseable label, comply or refuse, optionally safe redirect, and only then replies.

Because the label is a token span, the reward can be a string comparison against whether the conversation is known to be harmful or benign, with no judge model. Training uses G R P O on the final turn only, with a guard that the reply be non-empty, since otherwise the policy could just refuse forever. An external-judge reward variant is also tested.

Results on Llama three point one, eight billion and Qwen three, eight billion, across fourteen benchmarks. Average safe rate over five safety suites rises from fifty-nine point nine to ninety point four percent for the self-verified variant on Llama, and from forty-six point one to ninety-three point four on Qwen. The strongest baseline on Llama, a representation-level defence, reaches eighty-six point three. On the human-crafted multi-turn suite, the Qwen self-verified model reaches seventy-five point two against twenty-two point three for the base.

Costs. Utility benchmarks are flat, but the authors note those suites are scored in a format largely insensitive to safety training. The real price is over-refusal: on XSTest it rises from ten to twenty point four percent on Llama and from seven point two to twenty-six point four on Qwen. Ablations show the decision line carries the defence. Removing it was the most damaging change, and under human red-teaming dropped safety below the undefended base. The authors write that the commitment, not the deliberation before it, carries the defence.

The governance read. An auditable, machine-readable decision before the reply gives auditors something a rule can check, and a judge-free training reward weakens the circularity of training and evaluating against the same classifier. But the evidence is narrower than the headline: eight billion parameter models, static scripted attacks, the same attack families in training and test. Judges agree only moderately. The confidence intervals show separation from most baselines but not a resolved ordering against the strongest on every suite. The authors' own depth probe shows protection fails if an attacker can write into the assistant channel. They also disclose each baseline needed a configuration fix on Qwen, a reproducibility point and a possible source of bias. Little to say about frontier deployment.

Do agents have taste

Category: Evaluations and Benchmarks. rTaste, by Art Kanke at the University of Minnesota, asks whether research agents have taste, meaning judgment about which experiments are worth running before the outcome is known. It is a good question for anyone tracking how far R and D automation reaches past execution.

Six frontier models each read seven recent natural language processing papers and proposed five minimum viable experiments per paper, once alone and once as a team. Each registered a hypothesis and a pass threshold, forecast the probability of passing, and ranked the five. A separate executor, on one A one hundred G P U for at most forty-five minutes, ran everything, including the experiments ranked last. Three model auditors judged the results.

Of four hundred and fifteen experiments, a hundred and ninety-four were sound, valuable and new, but only seventy-three of those were also beyond the seed paper and non-obvious. Sixty-six percent of delivered experiments built on the seed paper, twenty-four percent checked its claims, and five percent opened a new direction. Two of the models proposed about two thirds of the beyond-the-paper work.

On judgment: the agents' forecasts beat a base rate, with Brier scores of about zero point one nine against zero point two four, but were not measurably better than those of a fresh copy of the same model given only the paper and the registrations. The agent's first choice was the auditors' favourite in thirty-eight percent of cases against twenty percent for a random pick, but its edge over simply taking the experiment written first was three points, with an interval running from minus eight to plus fifteen. Teams added cost more than clear quality. A run cost about fifteen dollars for a single researcher and twenty-four for a team.

The limits are real. Every label comes from models, there is no human baseline, auditors saw outcomes and two of the three are also evaluated models, and each condition ran once. The governance read: this is direct if thin evidence that the agenda-setting step is not yet cleanly delegable. It also flags evaluator self-preference, since judges from the same provider agreed more, a risk for any model-graded evaluation regime. And proposing is cheap and varies about a hundredfold between models, so oversight of research-agent budgets may matter more than execution costs. Model names and dates are as the paper states them.

Voice cloning features

Category: Interpretability. Discovering Interpretable Acoustic Features in Autoregressive Voice-Cloning Text-to-Speech Models, by Anuj Diwan, David Harwath and Eunsol Choi. It is a methods paper with no governance content. The authors apply sparse autoencoders, P C A directions and random directions to the residual stream of Qwen three T T S, plus five other models, and test probing, steering during generation, and automatic labelling of features.

Findings in brief. Pitch, energy, noise, accent and emotion are encoded, but only some concepts are predictable from a few features. Strong probing does not reliably translate into steering: the rank correlation between sparse probing score and steering effect is only about zero point two. Sparse autoencoders win on sparse probing of human-labelled concepts and on discovering new ones, while P C A is competitive for dense probing and steering. In the labelling test, an audio language model validated ninety-two of five hundred sampled sparse autoencoder features, against seventeen for random directions and one for P C A. Extended to all eligible features, six hundred and eighty-four validated, mostly speaker clusters, styles and recording conditions. A human check gave sixty-four point four percent label fit for validated features against fifty-one percent for the rest, a real but modest gap, and the same audio model labelled and judged, which the authors flag as circular.

For you, the useful part is evaluation discipline. Probing accuracy, steering effect and auto-interpretability scores can disagree, and random directions can match sparse autoencoders on some metrics, which is a caution for any policy leaning on interpretability as an audit signal. The finding that some features steer pitch, noise and energy without retraining touches cloned-voice misuse, but the paper tests no detection or safeguards. Single seed, one hundred steering examples, English only.

The cost of long context

Category: Compute and Hardware. The Real Cost of Context, by Lars Krupp and colleagues at R P T U Kaiserslautern-Landau, argues that energy estimates for language model inference wrongly treat input length as negligible.

They profile Mistral Small, a mixture of experts model with a hundred and nineteen billion total and six billion active parameters, through Mistral's A P I, with inputs from ten thousand to two hundred thousand tokens and outputs from a hundred to two thousand. Prompts begin with a random nonce to defeat prefix caching, so prefill is genuinely recomputed. A quasi-Poisson model fitted on the grid shows decode time linear in output length, with about six point seven milliseconds per output token, while prefill has both a linear and a quadratic term in input length. The fit explains ninety-eight point eight five percent of deviance. On unseen inputs of about two hundred and ten thousand tokens, the largest prediction error across all setups was three point nine seven percent.

Energy can't be measured through an A P I, so they fit matched time and energy models on three local G P U setups, find time and energy track each other linearly, and use the closest match, two H two hundred G P Us, as a lower bound for the A P I. With a power usage factor of one point two applied, the estimate sits well above the EcoLogits calculator, which gives flat values of about zero point one two and zero point four nine watt-hours at five hundred and at two thousand output tokens, whatever the input length. The gap grows with input length.

Limits: one model, one provider, three local setups, none an exact match, batch size one, and no public code or data yet. The time-to-energy mapping assumes similar power behaviour, which is untested. The governance read: measurement. Disclosure rules and thresholds that key on output tokens or short prompts can undercount long-context agentic use, and the quadratic term makes it worse as windows grow. Also interesting is the outside-in technique, inferring a provider's hidden hardware class from request timing alone. Note this is about inference energy, not training compute or chip controls.

Steering the connectives

Category: AI Capabilities. Guided-RLVR, from Beihang University, tackles collapse under reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards, where sampled attempts concentrate on a narrow set of reasoning paths. A small learned controller, at uncertain sentence boundaries during rollouts, boosts a connective such as wait, therefore or but until its probability reaches about one half. Controller and policy train together, with a small bonus for chains that make the final answer more predictable.

On two Qwen models, two R L algorithms and five math and code benchmarks, it has the best average in all four pairings, by one point one to two point eight points over the strongest baseline. Gains are largest at high sampling budgets, for about twelve point six percent more G P U time. For example, on Qwen two point five, seven billion with G R P O, pass at one hundred and twenty-eight on A I M E twenty twenty-four goes from forty-six point six seven to fifty-three point three three.

Treat the evidence with care. A I M E has thirty problems, so one problem is three point three three points. A single run seems reported per cell. The controller gets pretraining on six hundred thousand reasoning-trace samples that baselines do not. And the vanilla Qwen three baselines look weak, since even the weaker exploration baselines more than double them. Pass at one on Qwen two point five is modest or absent.

The governance read: this is a capabilities result. It supports the point that pass at one understates what a model can do under heavy sampling, and that post-training recipes can shift pass at k for about thirteen percent more training compute, so dangerous-capability evaluations should report high k results. Four and seven billion models, two thousand training examples: a direction, not a size.

Why is anything conscious

Category: Non-AI, though filed as computer science. Why Is Anything Conscious, by Michael Timothy Bennett, Sean Welsh and Anna Ciaunica, is a formal and largely philosophical theory. It starts from the evolved, embodied organism and says physical states are attractive or repulsive to a self-organising system, so valence comes first and neutral properties like red are built later as learned causes of valence. Three orders of self follow. A first-order self, essentially reafference, grounds phenomenal consciousness. A second-order self, which models how an audience decodes you, grounds reportable access. A third-order narrative self supports trust. The conclusions: phenomenal consciousness without access is probably common, the reverse implausible, and a philosophical zombie impossible. The slogan is that death grounds meaning.

The formal results are small, and the support is three toy Monte Carlo simulations. One theorem amounts to the observation that adding a constraint shrinks a set. The leap to zombies being impossible depends on interpretive premises a critic can reject.

For governance, the relevance is indirect. The account implies today's software is not conscious, since consciousness needs an embodied, mortal, self-organising system, which is a counterweight in AI welfare debates. The point that decoder mismatch can make a real mind look like noise is a neat one about evaluation validity. Low priority unless digital minds are in scope.
