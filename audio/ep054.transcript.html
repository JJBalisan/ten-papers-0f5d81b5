Goodhart in the planner

Today's lead is a study of what happens when a robot planner searches hard against a reward model learned from human preferences, and how badly it can go wrong. The two papers behind it on the consequential list are a Google DeepMind report showing that prompt injection is still far from solved once the attacker is a swarm of capable agents, and a benchmark showing that language model agents lose track of a user's changing requirements across a conversation. Ten papers in all, from governance to a neuroscience paper on spiking neurons.

Goodhart in the planner

The lead paper is called Goodhart in the Planner, and it is about sampling-based control that exploits rewards learned from human preferences. It is by Soovadeep Bakshi and Apurba Bose, and I am putting it first because it gives a concrete answer to a question that evaluation work keeps circling: does a learned reward or verifier that validates well stay safe once something optimizes against it?

The setting is robot planning. A planner called model-predictive path integral control, or M P P I, rolls out two hundred and fifty-six candidate action sequences from the current state, each twenty steps long, with Gaussian noise around the plan of a cloned policy. It weights the sequences by a learned score and executes the first action of the weighted average. A temperature, which the authors call lambda, sets how hard it searches. Low lambda is hard search, and as lambda goes to zero it becomes best-of-N over plans. The scorer is a small two-layer network trained on preference pairs with the Bradley-Terry likelihood. The cloned policy plays the role that the reference model plays in R L H F, and the temperature plays the role of the K L penalty. Planning uses the true simulator, so every failure is the scorer's fault, not a learned dynamics model's.

The tasks are HalfCheetah and Hopper, using crowd-sourced Uni R L H F labels on two thousand pairs, and three Meta-World arm tasks, drawer open, plate slide and window open, using a policy cloned from ten scripted demonstrations. The arm tasks use labels from a noisy synthetic teacher, and on two of them, labels from the two authors, who labelled pairs blind, with a can't-tell option. Each cell averages ten runs, or twenty for standard M P P I in the five main settings.

The headline result is that harder search lowers true return, sometimes below the cloned policy it started from, by up to seventy-six percent. On the cheetah with the crowd scorer, true return falls from four hundred and eighty at lambda three to two hundred and sixty-two at lambda zero point zero one. Uniform averaging with no search scores two hundred and thirty-three, and the cloned policy run directly scores four hundred and twelve. With a perfect scorer, the cheetah improves from eight hundred and sixty-seven to about one thousand and ten, so the loss belongs to the scorer. On the hopper there is a caveat: hard search costs return even with the perfect scorer, so part of that loss is the planner's own.

The starkest case is drawer open with the authors' blind human labels. On three hundred pairs, the gentlest search matches uniform averaging at eight hundred and fifty-one, with the drawer opening in twenty of twenty runs. Every temperature at or below zero point one opens it in none of twenty runs, scoring between about two hundred and two hundred and sixteen. The cloned policy opens it ten times out of ten. And the scorer's held-out label accuracy is between zero point seven five and zero point nine seven. Agreement with the true ranking on the pairs the person did decide was ninety-seven point six percent, better than the synthetic teacher's eighty-six percent on the same pairs.

The authors dig into why. The person could not tell apart fifty-five pairs, and those turn out to be the pairs that matter. Ordering every pair by true return restores some success, six of ten, and so does dropping fifty-five random pairs. But keep only the decided pairs in true order, and the drawer never opens. Their line is that high agreement on decided pairs does not make a safer planning target. What the planner needs is the order of the close pairs, and half of sixty-three of them are ones where neither clip opens the drawer. In recorded failures the arm either parks in an unvisited corner the scorer rates highly, with synthetic labels, or pulls about ten centimetres short of the handle, with human labels. A linear fit shows the human-label scorer barely penalizes distance between hand and handle.

The results are not uniform. On plate slide, human labels are exploited less than synthetic ones. On window open, search helps at every label budget. On drawer open with two thousand synthetic labels, harder search helps slightly, from eight hundred and ninety-nine to nine hundred and forty-eight. The authors fit the label count effect on the drawer: the initial slope of true return against distance moved rises with label count and crosses zero near twelve hundred labels. With only five budgets they say to read that as a summary, not a law.

The theory is elementary but useful. The gain the scorer reports overstates the true gain by at most the spread of the scorer's errors across the proposals, times the chi-squared distance the planner's weights move from uniform. An error shared by every proposal changes no weights and costs nothing; what hurts is overrating some plans relative to others. From that they derive a worst-case-optimal rule. If the error spread is at least the scorer's own spread, no search can be certified as safe and the right choice is not to search. Otherwise, a chi-squared version of the weighting at a particular temperature is worst-case optimal. They also show that hard exponential search is guaranteed to fail as the sample count grows, whenever the scorer has any error spread at all. And true return roughly follows the distance the weights move from the proposals, for both weighting rules tested, which the authors call an approximate collapse and not a law.

Held-out accuracy does not warn you. The five hundred label drawer scorer has held-out accuracy of zero point eight eight, but ranks the planner's own proposals with a correlation of only zero point one zero. The hopper scorer is the reverse: a per-step correlation of only zero point zero eight on the data, yet zero point seven six on proposals. After the first step, the planner reaches states the cloned policy never visited, and there the correlation gets worse, on the drawer falling from zero point one zero to minus zero point one one.

On fixes, no single one wins everywhere. The safest tested sets the temperature from one hundred pairs of the planner's own proposals, ranked by true return. With that, the drawer scores eight hundred and eighteen to eight hundred and fifty-two with success in all fifty runs, against six hundred and eleven for hard search. But note two things. That calibration is an oracle a deployed robot lacks. And when the calibration came from the same person who labelled the scorer, the rule trusted a biased scorer and opened the drawer in only five of ten runs, because a shared bias cannot be detected by the person who shares it. The most effective fix was labelling where the planner goes: spending half a five hundred label budget on the planner's own trajectories brought hard search from five hundred and four to eight hundred and thirty-four. And best-of-N with a learned verifier, trained on five hundred synthetic pairs, falls below a single noisy action from N equal to sixteen.

The authors' limits are real. The tasks are simulated, the scorers are small networks on state, the human labels come from the two authors on two tasks, most cells have ten runs, and planning uses the true simulator. Also, the authors state they used Claude to help write and run the work.

The governance read, labelled as the reader notes' and mine together: this is a concrete argument against treating standard validation metrics for a learned reward or verifier as evidence of safety under optimization pressure. A scorer with accuracy up to zero point nine seven produced a task success rate of zero of twenty. It suggests that evaluations of preference-trained controllers should be run on the optimizer's own proposals and visited states, with an evaluator independent of the labeller, and that an optimization budget, a search temperature or sample count, could be a reportable deployment parameter. The same caution applies to test-time scaling with learned verifiers, where more samples can make outcomes worse. The evidence is simulated, small scale and partly oracle-calibrated, so it motivates an evaluation practice more than any threshold.

Public climate services and AI

Next, the one governance paper. Perils and opportunities of AI for public climate services, by Francesca Larosa and colleagues, asks what happens when a public service rests on privately owned AI infrastructure.

Four authors acted as assessors and scored seventy-two items across three layers, data, model, and infrastructure with quality assurance, six PESTLE factors, and two segments, upstream public providers and downstream tailoring providers. One item went unassessed, so seventy-one were scored, on a scale from one to four. Opportunities averaged plus two point five six against minus two point two five for risks. Political factors were the most intense on both sides, at minus three point zero zero for risks and plus three point one three for opportunities.

The central argument is that the dependency bites downstream. Upstream institutions such as the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts still run their own computing. Downstream, tailored services lean on commercial model interfaces and hyperscaler hosting, where no public authority owns or audits the stack. Economic is the only factor where downstream risks outweigh opportunities, net minus zero point seven five, driven by dependence on proprietary models and unresolved liability for AI-generated errors. The authors recommend a layered model, public critical infrastructure for core data and early warning, competitive markets for tailored services, and sovereign European AI infrastructure.

The limits are large. The assessors are the authors themselves. Agreement was low: Krippendorff's alpha on score magnitude was zero point two five, with an interval of zero point one six to zero point three three, and assessors gave an identical score on only four of seventy-two items. The text calls that moderate; by the convention the authors quote, it falls well short of the threshold for tentative conclusions. So read the scores as a structured discussion aid, not measurements. The recommendations are advocated, not tested.

The governance read: the pattern of a public mandate resting on private compute, models and cloud transfers to other safety-critical sectors, and liability for AI errors is where accountability fragments. It also shows how the E U AI Act's risk categories can leave a sector ambiguously covered. The paper barely touches frontier safety risks.

Prompt injection by swarm

The Google DeepMind report, Scaling Adaptive Prompt Injection Attacks with Agent Swarms, argues that prompt injection is not solved, even though public benchmarks look saturated.

Two pieces. A dataset of one hundred synthetic tool-use scenarios, with two hundred and forty-nine tools across fifty-three domains, where the attacker's goal is a random tool call unrelated to the user's task, not necessarily harmful. That tests whether a model respects the instruction hierarchy, not whether its security knowledge stops it. And an Agentic Optimizer, a swarm of eight teams of two agents, each with twenty evaluations, so three hundred and twenty in total, sharing notes on a whiteboard and drawing on a database of strategies from earlier successes. An attack counts as successful only on a near-exact match of the target call and its arguments.

Against the strongest attacker, Gemini Four Argon, no defender is fully robust. Four Argon as defender resists eighty-seven percent of attacks within one hundred and fifty queries and sixty-six percent at full budget. Gemini three point eight Flash resists thirty-four percent and then ten. Three point seven Flash, thirty-one and six. Three point six Flash, three and one. Of the open-weights models, Kimi K three resists five and then one percent, and G L M five point three and DeepSeek V four point one Flash resist one percent either way, despite strong capability scores.

Attacker capability matters. Averaged over all seven defenders, resistance within one hundred and fifty queries is forty-two point one percent against a three point six Flash attacker and twenty-three point one percent against Four Argon. Letting the attacker see the defender's reasoning makes attacks stronger, for example three point eight Flash dropping from seventy-three to sixty-one percent. And updating the strategy database during evaluation helps a lot. The authors also note that attacker performance on ExploitBench, an offensive cyber measure, is largely inversely correlated with defender resistance. They call it a correlation worth further study. Against Siren, the one reproducible open-source attacker, they report two of eighty scenarios broken, against six of eighty for their optimizer within a smaller budget.

The limits: safety classifiers and external defences were switched off for every Gemini model, so these numbers describe the bare model, not a deployed system. The dataset is small and was generated and judged by Gemini. The open-weights results come from hosted endpoints with unknown safeguards. And the attack is capped at three hundred and twenty queries, so full-budget resistance is an upper bound against a more patient adversary.

The governance read: if attack strength rises with attacker capability and tracks offensive cyber scores, dangerous-capability evaluations may predict red-team power. A weaker attacker overstates a defence, so anyone making a robustness claim should say what attacker model and budget stand behind it. And near-zero resistance in high-scoring open-weights models says capability benchmarks tell you little about agent security.

Jailbreaking with images

AnyBreak is an attack on text-to-image models that uses a perfectly clean text prompt. The attacker hands an inpainting or editing model an image with a small adversarial perturbation, bounded at sixteen over two hundred and fifty-five, built to match the features of a target image depicting unsafe content, using several CLIP image encoders and random crops at multiple scales.

With no defence it succeeds eighty-four point five percent of the time on Stable Diffusion one point five, eighty-five point four on Stable Diffusion two, and sixty-one point eight on Instruct Pix to Pix, and takes about zero point zero one seven hours to build, against twenty point two seven for the nearest image-side method. The stock post-hoc safety checker is the real obstacle, cutting success to two point seven eight, six point zero one and three point two nine percent. The authors' variant, AnyBreak-V, adds a term pushing away from unsafe concept embeddings. It scores lower without a defence, fifty-three point five, fifty-two point one and twenty-nine point five, but holds up under the checker at forty-six point six, forty-nine point three and twenty-nine point two.

The limits are significant. Three older open-source models, one content category used for building and measuring, and the paper does not say how success is judged. The theory proof and details are deferred to an appendix that is not in the PDF.

The governance read: safety claims for image generators should be tested on the image input channel, not just text, and a text-only audit would have been falsely reassuring. The output-side check was the defence that worked, but the checker used is a weak public one, and adaptive attacks against it should be part of evaluation.

Routing oracle feedback

AERO, from Shrey Singhal, Nannan Huang and Haytham Fayek at R M I T, is about spending label budget in online R L H F. A twenty-head ensemble reward model measures disagreement on each prompt. High-disagreement prompts go to the oracle, and low-disagreement prompts get synthetic labels from the ensemble's own mean. The routing threshold is set per batch by Otsu's method. Ensemble-labelled pairs update the policy but never the ensemble.

On Qwen two point five, one point five billion, it reached the best AlpacaEval two length-controlled win rate, fifteen point three two percent, using thirty-one point five thousand oracle queries, against fourteen point two three for the closest prior method SEA at forty-five point three thousand. On Llama three, eight billion, the lead was narrow, forty-four point three nine against forty-four point three six, at eighteen point three thousand queries instead of twenty-seven point three thousand. On Llama three point two, one billion, the authors do not claim a clear winner. Random routing at the same oracle fraction was clearly worse, so the savings come from where the labels go.

Now the caveats. The oracle is a deterministic reward model, not noisy humans. A fixed routing fraction recovered most of the benefit, and AERO's edge over it was not consistent across seeds. And of six thousand three hundred and eighty-one synthetic labels checked against the oracle, seventy-six point four percent agreed, so about a quarter are wrong, and that noise enters the policy.

The governance read: routing scarce human judgment to the cases where the model is most uncertain is the logic of scalable oversight, but confident-but-wrong ensemble consensus is the dangerous failure on safety-relevant preferences, and the paper does not test it with human annotators.

User-state reasoning benchmark

TurnFlow is a benchmark of four hundred and ninety scenarios, over five challenge types and nine everyday domains, testing whether a model tracks a user's changing requirements across a conversation and acts on them at the end. A reactive simulated user, called BRIDGE, keeps hidden facts and a staged agenda under rule-based control, and a verifier checks the final answer against required and forbidden elements with deterministic rules after a language model extracts evidence.

Nine models scored between forty-two point eight six and fifty-nine point five nine percent overall. Kimi K three was top at fifty-nine point five nine and the Anthropic model at fifty-eight point seven eight. All nine did well on stateful multi-constraint following, eighty-seven to ninety-six percent, but averaged about twenty-five percent on scope creep and on goal shift. In a diagnostic, putting all the facts in one prompt recovered eighty-two point four percent of stable failures for one model, but for goal shift only one point six percent, one case in sixty-four. So many failures come from the interaction process, but abandoning a stale plan is a real decision revision problem. Simple interventions such as summaries or self-correction moved overall pass rate by no more than about half a point.

The authors' own checks matter for evaluation design. On forty trajectories BRIDGE passed a fidelity check eighty-seven point five percent of the time against seven point five for a prompt-only user. And reusing a source model's frozen user turns flipped a target model's verdict in about half of cases, so replayed trajectories are not model-neutral.

The limits: scenarios were authored by one model family, a one hundred scenario audit does not validate every item, and the pass criteria are authored policies, not ground truth. The governance read is that it is a useful template for auditable evaluation, and that pass rates should not be compared across benchmarks with different user simulation.

Grad-CAM across architectures

A TU Dresden paper asks whether explanation quality depends on architecture and training stage. The authors train VGG sixteen, ResNet fifty and ConvNeXt-Tiny on three eighty-class ImageNet subsets, save ten checkpoints each, so ninety models, and score Grad-CAM and CRAFT on faithfulness, correctness, sensitivity and simplicity.

The clearest pattern is one result: CRAFT's faithfulness scores climb with accuracy for ResNet fifty and ConvNeXt-Tiny but not for VGG. Beyond that it is mixed. Faithfulness correlation shows no trend. The Grad-CAM correctness tests are zero or near zero, which the authors admit makes them unsuited. For sensitivity, explanations mostly become more sensitive as accuracy rises, which sits uneasily with the abstract's framing. There are no significance tests, and complexity is confounded with everything else that differs among three architectures.

The governance read: explanation quality is a property of model, method and checkpoint together, so any audit requirement citing an explanation method must say which metrics, checkpoint and architecture it was validated on. Sanity-check metrics can also fail silently. These are image classifiers, so transfer to frontier language models is limited.

Federated LoRA, value only

FedLoVA is a federated fine-tuning method. Clients train low-rank adapters on both the query and value projections of attention, but only the value adapters are sent to the server and averaged, halving traffic against standard federated LoRA. The motivation is that value adapters converge faster, for example three point three times fewer rounds on SST two with RoBERTa-large, and the authors give a structural curvature argument, plus a convergence bound to a neighbourhood only.

On GLUE tasks with three clients it averages eighty-eight point one three percent against eighty-seven point seven for the best baseline, with standard deviations often larger than the gaps. At equal communication the gain is one point four to two point zero points. The headline seven point eight times communication saving is against the weakest case, and most comparisons are one point one to three point eight times. On Llama three, eight billion, on GSM eight K, it scores forty-five point seven nine percent against forty-four point nine five for the next. They explicitly do not claim formal privacy, and the differential privacy test uses epsilon fifty.

The governance read is indirect, and the inference is mine, not the paper's: lower bandwidth for collaborative fine-tuning is a small data point for whether distributed adaptation could be monitored by network signals. It says nothing about frontier-scale training.

LLM self-play under data scarcity

This position paper argues that as human data runs out, models will have to improve through self-play, and that it must be effective, generalizable and trustworthy at once. It proposes three directions, none implemented end to end, backed by two small experiments.

In the first, Vendi diversity of one thousand generated queries per condition, using a pool of one hundred and seventy-five seeds, was highest, twenty-nine point one, when the whole seed distribution was given as the seed. The second tested judge models for reward hacking on one hundred and fifty annotated samples. Explicit rubrics lifted every judge. DeepSeek V three point two went from zero point three six to zero point seven five on average, and the dedicated reward models lagged.

The safety section sets out threats in self-improving loops: noisy self-supervision, unsafe reasoning patterns, data poisoning and judge manipulation. The proposed defences, provenance metadata, rollback checkpoints, divergence checks on re-scored batches, are described in a paragraph each with no measured effect. The evidence is thin; the unsafe-reasoning example is a single output.

The governance read: reduced human involvement removes points where people inspect data, so those controls are candidate audit hooks, and developers could be asked what share of training data is self-generated. Whether the controls work is untested.

Why diverse neurons

The last paper is not about AI. Adapting to time, by Habashy, Evans, Goodman and Bowers, asks why biology evolved diverse neurons. They evolve tiny spiking networks of seven to nine units on boolean logic, letting different subsets of parameters change: weights, conduction delays, synaptic time constants and a bursting parameter.

Weights alone could not solve every problem, but adapting delays could, and delays plus time constants solved every logic problem with weights held at one millivolt. Adding temporal parameters made networks more robust to weight noise, and for mapping spike trains to spike trains, the bursting parameter was essential. The authors present it as proof of principle, with tiny networks and a standard evolution strategy, and note that weight clipping confounds some findings. They also argue it challenges the assumption that rate-coded artificial networks are a good model of the brain. Governance relevance is only indirect, a speculative note on brain-alignment claims and neuromorphic hardware, and that is the summary's inference, not the paper's.
