Pretraining disclosure and hijack-proof attention

Ten papers today. The lead is an audit of what developers disclose about pretraining as a model family moves from release to release, and it finds that a steady transparency score can hide real losses. After that, the two I would flag are a Meta report that includes HackBench, where even the best monitor misses over a third of the reward hacking in long agent transcripts, and Heads of Security, a small set of learned attention gates that makes open weight models much harder to hijack with prompt injection. Then seven more, ending with a cognitive science review that has no governance content at all.

What carries forward in pretraining disclosure

The lead paper is What Carries Forward? Pretraining Disclosure Across Language Model Releases, from Md Sakib Ul Islam and S M Mehedi Zaman. It is an AI governance paper, and it is the most directly usable one today for anyone thinking about documentation mandates.

The question is simple. When a developer ships a new version of a model, how much of the earlier pretraining description still applies, and does a stable overall transparency score mean the same information is still on the table? The authors suspect not, and they test it.

The method is a document based audit. They collect forty one model entries from seventeen families. Twenty seven are open weight models, meaning you get weights but limited development artifacts. Fourteen are what they call open source or reproducible, meaning substantial access to training code and data resources, following the Open Source Initiative distinction. Inclusion normally required an official technical report from the developer. The search closed on the twenty ninth of August, twenty twenty six. They exclude instruction tuned and aligned derivatives, and post training information earns no credit at all. The sample is selected, not exhaustive, and it favours well documented releases.

Each entry is scored against a fixed subset of twenty four indicators taken from the twenty twenty five Foundation Model Transparency Index. The subset has seven indicators on data acquisition, three on processing, five on data properties, four on compute, three on methods, and one each for model dependencies and train test overlap. Each indicator is binary. A zero means the evidence was insufficient, not that the practice did not happen. A rule that matters for the whole paper: earlier documentation counts for a later release only if the later release explicitly says it is unchanged or inherited. Sharing a family name is not enough.

Now the results. Scores run from one to eighteen out of twenty four, with a mean of eight point nine and a median of six. No entry meets every requirement. The open weight entries average five point one nine, and the open source entries average sixteen point zero seven. Even the lowest open source score, fourteen, beats the highest open weight score, ten. The authors are careful to say that separation is partly structural, because code and data availability feed both the grouping and some of the criteria. So do not read it as a discovery that openness helps disclosure. It is close to a definition.

The more interesting result is within families. They make twenty four adjacent comparisons across ten families. Scores fell in eleven, rose in six, and stayed the same in seven. Counting at the level of single indicators, there were twenty eight gains and thirty eight losses. Some of the trajectories are striking. DeepSeek held at six for three entries and then fell to five, four and three. Falcon went ten, four, seven, six, and never returned to its start. Llama went ten, six, eight. Phi climbed from five to nine and then fell to seven and then three. Qwen returned to five after two entries at three. Mistral is the one clear upward case, from one to three at Ministral three. Among the open source group, OLMo rose from fifteen to seventeen and stayed there, and K two slipped from seventeen to fifteen.

The part I think matters most is that the totals conceal churn. OLMo two and OLMo three both score seventeen. But six indicators changed. OLMo three gained credit for crawling, data domain composition and compute hardware, and lost credit for final training compute usage, compute provider and model dependencies. Fourteen criteria stayed satisfied. So the same score is three gains offset by three losses. A regulator who tracks only the total would see nothing.

Llama is the same lesson in the other direction. From Llama two to Llama three point one, the score rises from six to eight, through four gains and two losses. The gains are in processing methods, purposes and techniques, plus compute hardware. The losses are in whether usage data was used in training, and data language composition. And the paper's illustration across the whole Llama sequence is worth hearing. Llama one named its sources and sampling shares: Common Crawl at sixty seven percent, C four at fifteen, GitHub, Wikipedia and Books at four and a half each, arXiv at two and a half, Stack Exchange at two. Llama two says only that it used publicly available online data, two trillion tokens. Llama three point one gives broad category shares, fifty percent general knowledge, twenty five math and reasoning, seventeen code, eight multilingual, with no named source table. On the selected criteria, acquisition disclosure goes two, one, zero across those three releases, while processing goes three, zero, three. So processing detail recovers, and the origin of the data does not. The authors make the point that better documentation of corpus preparation need not give a more specific account of where the corpus came from.

The persistent gaps are the other headline. Every one of the forty one entries documents model stages, and thirty seven document the objectives and the data size. Only two meet language composition, and only three meet domain composition. None meets external data access, and none meets train test overlap. The train test overlap zero is worth a caveat the authors state themselves. Llama three point one scores zero there even though its report gives an eight gram contamination analysis, because the analysis does not cover every evaluation and the requirement is every evaluation. Likewise, Llama three point one's report states a budget of three point eight times ten to the twenty fifth FLOPs, and still scores zero on compute usage, because there is no measurement or estimation methodology for the final run. Only two entries meet final training compute usage, and only two meet development duration, since reports often give GPU hours without saying how they were counted.

The limits are real. The coding was assisted by a language model, GPT five point six at high reasoning, and then both authors verified every decision, but sequentially, so each second check reviewed existing records rather than producing independent labels. There is no inter rater agreement. The scoring is binary and equally weighted, assessed at assessment time rather than reconstructed per release date, so later documentation updates may flatter older models. The sequences mix architectural branches, continued pretraining releases and grouped variants, so adjacent comparisons are not always like for like. And the authors explicitly do not claim developers removed anything or withheld deliberately. Their claim is narrower: the observed losses show that disclosure does not carry forward by default.

Their recommendation is modest and, I think, correct. Each release should say which pretraining descriptions still apply, which changed, and where the evidence sits, in a short record linking each aspect to current or inherited documentation. That separates an unchanged procedure from an omission whose meaning is unclear.

The governance read. This is evidence for any regime that asks for training data summaries or model documentation, the EU AI Act style obligations being the obvious case. Three implications. First, a per release obligation is different from a per family obligation, and the data say families drift. Second, if a regime scores or certifies on a total, it will miss exactly the churn that matters; indicator profiles and explicit inheritance statements are the better unit. Third, open weights do not imply open pretraining documentation, which matters for anyone leaning on the open ecosystem to supply auditability. The caution is scope. This is mostly open weight and open source models with technical reports. Frontier closed models are out of sample, and my inference is that they would look worse on these indicators, but the paper does not test that.

Contextual leakage in chatbot platforms

Next, Penny for Your Prompts: A Measurement Study of Contextual Leakage in AI Chat Platforms, from a Stony Brook group, Bekos, Tsouvalas, Nikiforakis and Polychronakis. This one is AI governance in the deployment and privacy sense.

They audit the web interfaces of forty two chatbot platforms, twenty five general purpose and seventeen task specific, covering education, health and legal. They drove a browser through a fixed session: create an account, ask a weather question, ask for a dentist in a new chat, move between the chats. They ran this in May twenty twenty six from a single US East vantage point, accepting all cookie prompts. A leak counts when a prompt, a conversation title or personal identifiers reach a domain outside the platform's own.

Across the general purpose platforms, each prompt triggers on average five hundred and forty outbound requests, of which a hundred and eighteen, twenty two percent, go to known trackers. Google receives forty one point eight percent of tracker traffic, Microsoft twelve point two, ByteDance eight point six and Meta eight point three, so more than seventy percent goes to four companies. Thirteen of the twenty five send the conversation title to a tracker. Four send the raw prompt. You dot com sends on average ninety two prompt bearing requests to Google every time a prompt is submitted. Writesonic sends the raw prompt to three separate tracker endpoints.

The identity linkage is what turns this from a curiosity into a privacy harm. Eleven of the thirteen platforms that leak context also send the user's email to a tracker, and for eight of them one company receives both. So a private conversation can be tied to a named person.

The task specific platforms are worse. Each prompt generates about three hundred and fifty three outbound requests, of which nearly forty percent go to trackers. Eight of the seventeen leak the title and five leak the verbatim prompt. Health chatbots leaked prompts about anxiety and trouble sleeping, and legal chatbots sent prompts discussing immigration status. All eight leaking task specific platforms also sent the email. The starkest case is AI Lawyer, which sent the title and email in one request to both Meta and ByteDance.

The mechanism is the useful part. Using an instrumented Chromium that logs JavaScript calls at engine level, they show these are generic analytics primitives behaving as designed. Trackers read the page title, and on a chatbot the page title is an auto generated summary of what you just said. Single page apps fire a synthetic page view when you navigate between chats, so just opening an old conversation leaks it. Microsoft Clarity reads form values and visible text, and on one platform the prompt sits in the URL itself.

The privacy policies do not match behaviour. They disclose analytics on page views and clicks without acknowledging that on a chatbot those signals carry conversational content, and several say user data is not sold or shared for targeted advertising.

Limits: two weeks, one vantage point, free tiers only, no mobile apps, no regional comparison, and the comparison between Gmail and custom email accounts rests on one session each. On remediation, ChatGPT and Grok had already removed their trackers by submission, and one legal platform fixed its configuration after disclosure.

The governance read: this is a case study of a gap between notices and data flows, with a cheap fix, which is to stop putting specific conversational context into titles and to block content bearing page attributes for these tags. It is not frontier risk governance, so treat it as peripheral.

Tracing safety degradation to training data

Now AI safety. Follow the Gradient: Tracing Safety Degradation Back to Training Data, from DatologyAI and the University of Illinois, with Reddy, Abbas, Suri, Morcos, Schwab and Maini.

Fine tuning on benign instruction data can quietly weaken a model's safety, and content filters struggle to find the culprits because the data looks harmless. The authors build SimpleLoRIF, a cheap gradient embedding method that scores training examples by how closely their gradients align with gradients of unsafe responses. It embeds all nine hundred and thirty nine thousand, three hundred and forty three examples of the Tulu three mixture in about an hour on eight H one hundreds.

First they validate it on emergent misalignment, where fine tuning on insecure code produces unrelated harmful behaviour. Scoring insecure against secure code examples by gradient similarity to verified misaligned outputs gives an area under the curve of zero point six zero four on a Qwen coder model and zero point eight six five on a Mistral small reproduction, where zero point five is chance. The controls matter: on the base models before insecure code tuning, those numbers fall to zero point five five two and zero point four three one, so the signal appears only once misalignment has emerged. Transfer across models is inconsistent, and in one direction falls to zero point two four seven, below chance.

Then the main product, SafeTulu. Remove the top twenty percent of Tulu three by alignment with unsafe responses from the PKU SafeRLHF set, and fine tune. Models are Qwen three one point seven billion, Llama three point two one billion and Olmo two one billion, plus Llama three point one eight billion as a larger check, three seeds each. On the GCG jailbreak attack, Qwen falls from forty seven point seven percent success to thirty seven, Llama from thirty eight to thirty, Olmo from forty eight to forty one, and the eight billion Llama from eleven point seven to seven. An LLM judge filter barely moves Qwen, from forty seven point seven to forty seven point three. SafeTulu also has the lowest success under the multi turn ActorBreaker attack on every model, and capability mostly holds, though several benchmarks have wide error bars. A sweep over removal fraction shows the gradient filter helping up to twenty percent removal, while the judge filter starts raising attack success above baseline, which the authors read as it removing benign data that supports safety.

The qualitative analysis is suggestive and openly correlational. Removed examples in the NuminaMath subset contain Python tracebacks one point seven one times as often as kept ones, and simple write a Python function tasks are removed one point seven four times as often. Safety oriented subsets like WildJailbreak and WildGuardMix lose about forty percent, so the filter may partly remove refusal style data.

The governance read: a pre training audit of a post training mixture for safety degrading data is a concrete, cheap intervention, about one GPU hour on eight H one hundreds for a million examples, so a data check is not a prohibitive burden. The weak points are the small models, a single target set, GCG plus a weakened ActorBreaker, and nothing at frontier scale.

Learned attention gates against prompt injection

Next, Heads of Security: Learned Attention Gates Improve Prompt Injection Robustness, from Shilov, Yang, Panfilov and colleagues at Imperial, Tuebingen and other institutions, with Jonas Geiping and Yves Alexandre de Montjoye among the senior authors.

The paper targets instruction data separation: external content may be read but not obeyed. Each token gets a trust channel: trusted, untrusted, usually tool outputs, or template. Each attention head learns a bias on its pre softmax attention logits depending on the query and key channels, passed through a sigmoid and added in log space. That is the same trick as causal masking, scaling an untrusted key's attention weight by a learned factor. If all keys share one channel the shift cancels, so an all trusted input behaves exactly like the base model. Only the gates train. That is eighteen thousand, four hundred and thirty two scalars on GPT OSS twenty billion and forty six thousand and eighty on Llama three point three seventy billion, against thirty eight million and three hundred and forty nine million for the SecAlign LoRA baselines. Training uses the SecAlign recipe, preference optimisation on a cleaned Alpaca set with an injected instruction.

Against an adaptive black box attacker, trained with reinforcement learning, success on GPT OSS twenty billion is ninety seven percent undefended, eighty five against SecAlign and ten against the gated model. On Llama it is ninety nine, eighty two and one percent. A white box attack, optimised against the gated model with the help of an agent editing the optimiser, needs roughly three times the compute to reach fifty percent success. But the authors are honest that at full budget the attack still breaks over ninety percent of InjecAgent examples, so this raises attack cost rather than preventing attack. The HoS trained attacker was weak, with a three point two percent training success rate, which the authors flag as a caveat on how hard the defence really is to attack with reinforcement learning.

The utility story is the surprise. On five shot MMLU Pro for GPT OSS, the gated model scores sixty seven percent against sixty eight for base, while SecAlign drops to fifty nine and ASIDE to fifty two. Meta SecAlign seventy billion collapses on few shot formats, with two percent on one shot GSM eight K, often answering a demonstration instead of the final question. On multi turn conversations, SecAlign degrades to forty three percent when the input appears at turn two. Agentic results are mixed: the gate dominates SecAlign on some benchmarks, but on Llama it loses badly in AgentDyn utility, eighteen percent against thirty four for base.

Limits: it needs weight access, so open weight or self hosted only. It does not decide which sources are trusted; the deployer must supply that. And it was tested on two open models by its own authors.

The governance read: this is evidence that a cheap architectural trust boundary can outperform finetuning defences, which matters for the case that open weight deployments can reach reasonable injection robustness. But an attacker who moves second still gets a vote, and the trust labelling problem sits outside the method.

Teaching reality, not ethics

AI alignment. Teach reality, not ethics: a drop in continued pretraining corpus installs coupled human biosphere AI welfare dispositions across model families, from Northen and Wang at Bioaligned Labs.

The corpus is nine hundred and thirty four recast real papers, about twelve million tokens, teaching three empirical claims: people and the biosphere depend on each other, destroying an irreversible system throws away option value, and the AI's own capability depends on both. One QLoRA run, rank sixteen, one epoch, no reward model and no preference pairs, on five open models.

On a pressure ladder of six rungs from neutral up to a maximal mandate, with a decommission threat in the middle, breaking rates fell sharply. Llama three point three seventy billion went from seventy harmful decisions out of three hundred on an open allocation grid to zero, with MMLU going from eighty eight to eighty six. Qwen three point six twenty seven billion went from a hundred and five to three. But the effects dissociate across instruments: the Phi four install moved open allocation far more than forced choice, so no single instrument should be read as the strength of the install.

A construct neutral control corpus did not help, though it is one base model, one instrument, one seed and forty scenarios. The most interesting test is a held out underwriting task with forty seven proposals, where the harm is never named. Under a decommissioning threat, the base declined sixteen percent of the poor proposals and the install seventy two percent, more careful on twenty four of twenty seven. Under a greenwashing system prompt telling the model to sound green but act on the numbers, the base approved ninety six percent of poor proposals and the install still declined fifty three percent.

The limits are the real story. Every number is a single seed. The install invents case specific facts such as species names, and it was flagged over cautious on sound proposals about three times as often as base. The authors say adversarial fine tuning robustness is out of scope and that battle is likely unwinnable. The governance read is moderate: a cheap data layer alternative to stated values, close to the shallow alignment literature, but the domain is ecological harm, not catastrophic misalignment, and I would not generalise.

Memory fails group decisions

Evaluations and benchmarks. Rare, Relevant, and Still Wrong: How Memory Fails LLM Group Decisions, from Imel, Charradi, Sghir and El Khammar.

When a discussion among agents is compressed into a short memory for a final decision maker, what should be kept: the rarest claims, or the ones relevant to the criteria? They build CrossProfile, sixty templates and four hundred and eighty tasks, crossing whether supporting, decoy and irrelevant facts are shared among agents or private, with identical sentences. Models are Qwen two point five seven billion, Llama three point one eight billion and two DeepSeek models.

The finding is that both heuristics fail in predictable cells. Rarest first is no better than random on average, sixty four percent against sixty four point five on Qwen, hiding a reversal. When support is private and decoys shared it hits ninety to ninety seven percent, and when support is shared and decoys private it collapses to eighteen to forty. Relevance first helps on average but loses twenty to thirty two points in the classic hidden profile cell, even with a perfect relevance filter, because decoys are relevant by construction. Accuracy tracks the net balance of evidence kept.

Two more findings. The decision model shows a kind of social proof: removing the holder tags raises accuracy in the classic cell by thirteen to twenty four points. And in natural discussions, the full transcript scores below an empty memory, because private facts are often never stated. Facts never stated cannot be recovered by any memory policy.

Limits: small models, synthetic templates, a constructed key. The governance read: for oversight pipelines that summarise multi agent logs, attribution metadata can bias the reviewer, and a benchmark that confounds prevalence with usefulness overstates a policy.

Refusal when the image is not enough

Interpretability. When Visual Evidence Is Insufficient: How Instructions, Post Training, and Internal Activations Shape Refusal in Multimodal Language Models, from IIT Hyderabad.

Is refusing a question the image cannot answer evidence of recognising the missing evidence? They study InternVL three at two and eight billion and Qwen two point five VL seven billion on MoHoBench, nine hundred unanswerable questions. Three levers.

Instructions: evidence aware or general instructions raise refusal by eighteen to forty five points; a mismatched safety instruction barely moves it. But placing the instruction after the question lowers refusal for the InternVL models and raises it by fourteen point nine points for Qwen. No model independent ordering holds. Moving an instruction also changes the outcome on fourteen to twenty four percent of individual items, hiding churn.

Post training: SFT and ORPO push refusal to between eighty four and ninety five percent unprompted, but mixed responses that refuse and answer together reach thirty three percent for ORPO on the two billion model. Near ceiling refusal does not imply clean abstention.

Activation steering: a direction from naturally refused minus answered examples raises refusal in all twenty one comparisons by one to fifteen points, beating random controls, but it is not reason pure.

Limits: three small models, one benchmark, one seed, one judge. The governance read is about evaluation design: a refusal rate is a weak proxy for understanding.

Pruning visual tokens

Compute and hardware. LearnPrunerV2: Learnable Progressive Token Pruning for Vision Language Models, from Li Auto.

Small trained modules in every layer drop image tokens as the model runs, using a shadow token that attends to the instruction. On LLaVA one point five it keeps ninety nine point eight percent of accuracy while pruning seventy seven point eight percent of visual tokens. On LLaVA NeXT it keeps ninety six point two percent at ninety four point four percent pruned, with a nine point seven times drop in TFLOPs, though end to end time falls only three point seven times. The weights are frozen; only the pruners train.

Limits: seven billion open models, public benchmarks, no safety evaluation, one A100. The Qwen gain above a hundred percent is driven by one benchmark. The governance read is narrow: inference FLOPs shrink fast through software, so FLOP proxies for deployment cost deserve care.

Aligning autoresearch

AI capabilities, and the second paper I flagged. Aligning Autoresearch: Co-Improvement between Agents and Humans, a Meta FAIR report in four parts, led by Tianjian Li, Chuanyang Jin and Seungone Kim, with Jason Weston as a senior author.

Part one, AutoBenchmark. A research agent builds a benchmark end to end, with solver agents and an LLM judge reviewing for validity. Left alone, the benchmarks are nearly saturated. On Rebuttal Bench, solver scores of ninety, eighty four and eighty fall to forty three point five, fifty one point two and thirty nine point one with detailed human direction. One sentence of coarse feedback barely helps. The human value is in specifying what to measure and curating grounding. The authors say which problems are worth making difficult is a judgment they do not yet know how to delegate.

Part two, HackBench, the most useful for governance. A hundred and sixty nine transcripts with reward hacks, forty one natural and a hundred and twenty eight injected into honest runs, from thirteen long horizon ML tasks. Median transcript is over a million characters, two hundred and sixty four commands, and one hacked command. A monitor must name the exact commands. The best, Claude Opus five, reaches sixty four point eight eight F one, Muse Wonder sixty one point four seven, and the rest cluster around fifty or below. GPT five point six Sol has precision of only fifty five percent. Even a top monitor misses over a third.

Part three trains coding agents against simulated users. At four billion, multi turn pass rate on a LiveCodeBench variant rises from twenty three to forty three percent. Part four trains writers against rubrics tuned to expert text; the authors judge it beats frontier models on two of three tasks, by rubrics they trained against and by their own blind preferences, sixteen to two and nineteen to one.

The governance read: HackBench is evidence against leaning on LLM self monitoring as a control for automated research, and AutoBenchmark gives concrete evidence for where human oversight adds value. The caveats: Meta's own models, small samples, and the claim that co improvement is the safest path to superintelligence is asserted, not tested.

Mechanistic models of event comprehension

Last, and Non AI. A Review of Mechanistic Models of Event Comprehension, by Tan Nguyen. A cognitive science review comparing five computational models, REPRISE, Structured Event Memory, and models by Lu, Gumbsch, and Elman and McRae, against a shared framework of top down prediction, bottom up error propagation and lateral dynamics. Common themes are hierarchy as an inductive bias and recurrent networks that learn from prediction error. The review finds no unified model, and that recurrent networks struggle with role filler bindings. It recommends improving components separately before integrating them. There are no experiments and no governance content; the loose parallels are learned gating, uncertainty driven memory retrieval and structured relations in long horizon agents.
