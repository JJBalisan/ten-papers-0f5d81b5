When to stop testing

Today's lead is a statistical rule for deciding when pre-deployment testing has gone on long enough, from Rachel Li, Chris Hays, Aaditya Ramdas and Manish Raghavan. The next two are a user study showing that the assistant that holds correct answers under pressure is the one people rate worst, and a measurement paper on what it really costs to prove, cryptographically, that an AI computation was run as declared. Ten papers today, from governance and alignment through interpretability and hardware, with one brain decoding paper at the end.

Stopping rule for testing

The lead paper is called Controlling the Probability of Unknown Unknowns in Pre-Deployment Testing of AI Systems. It is filed under AI governance, and it earns the lead because it turns a vague judgement, that we have tested enough, into a rule you could write down before testing starts.

The question is this. A developer tests an agent on a stream of tasks and finds failures. Known failures can be fixed or covered by contingency plans. What the developer actually has to judge is whether the probability that the next test session reveals a failure nobody has seen yet is small enough to release. The authors treat that as a sequential version of a classic problem from nineteen fifty-three, the missing mass problem of Good and Turing. You draw items from a distribution, and you want the probability that the next item belongs to a class you have never seen. Here the items are agent outputs, and the unseen classes are failure modes.

The difficulty is that the developer will check this number again and again, and will stop when it looks good. A bound that is only valid at a fixed sample size breaks under that kind of peeking. So the authors build an anytime valid upper confidence sequence. At every step you get an upper bound on the unseen failure probability, and with probability at least one minus alpha, the bound holds at every step at once. That means it still holds at a data dependent stopping time. The stopping rule is simple: stop the first time the bound drops below your tolerance, epsilon.

The setup needs two functions. A failure indicator says whether an output contains a failure. An unseen indicator says whether that failure is new relative to the history of failures already observed. The definition of novelty is flexible. It can be a fixed taxonomy, a similarity threshold in an embedding space, or a dynamic rule such as, would the mitigations we already built have prevented this. The only requirements are that adding failures to the history never makes a seen failure unseen, and that an output already in the history is never unseen.

The method works by testing by betting. A gambler bets that the missing mass is below a threshold. While new failures keep appearing, the gambler loses. Once the true missing mass falls below the threshold, the gambler starts winning, and large wealth is evidence against the claim that the missing mass is still large. The unusual feature is that the gambler is expected to win eventually, so the wealth process is not a supermartingale forever, which is what standard proofs rely on. The authors get around this by using the fact that the missing mass only ever goes down. It is monotone non increasing, and for a countable output space it converges to zero almost surely. The countability matters. In an uncountable space, new events could keep appearing with probability bounded away from zero. Their key step is that the wealth process is a supermartingale for as long as the null hypothesis, that the missing mass is above the threshold, is still true, and it only stops being one at the moment the missing mass crosses the threshold, which is exactly when it is safe to halt.

The algorithm mixes betting processes in two ways. It mixes over start times, with slowly decaying weights on later starts, because early on many new failures would make a single betting process shrink exponentially and delay stopping. And it averages uniformly over the bet size, which approximates the best fixed bet in hindsight, in the sense of universal portfolio theory. Inverting the mixture gives the upper bound.

Three theoretical results matter. First, validity: the true missing mass stays below the bound at all times simultaneously, via Ville's inequality. Second, the stopping delay is logarithmic. If the missing mass first falls below some level gamma under epsilon at time T gamma, then with high probability the procedure stops within a number of extra rounds that grows only logarithmically in T gamma. The expected delay is of order the log of T gamma plus two. Third, a matching lower bound. If the missing mass is only just below epsilon, no valid procedure can promise any finite delay, because a nearby distribution just above epsilon is statistically indistinguishable. And when the missing mass is a fixed distance below epsilon, any valid procedure needs an expected delay at least logarithmic in T gamma, so the logarithmic delay is optimal up to a constant factor. The authors also note that without assumptions on the distribution, any valid nontrivial bound has to depend on the data, because a data independent bound would have to be one.

Now the extensions, which are where governance practice gets touched. Testers rarely sample from the deployment distribution. Beta testers, or deliberate oversampling of under tested task categories, give a different sampling distribution. If the ratio between the target and sampling distributions is known and bounded, inverse propensity weighting handles it. In the experiment, sampling short sessions at twice the rate of long ones delayed stopping modestly, on the order of one hundred to two hundred samples, and coverage stayed valid, zero point nine seven three under the deployment distribution and zero point nine eight zero under the shifted sampler at three thousand sessions.

The second extension is imperfect labels. Having humans label every session is expensive, and an LLM judge that misses true failures would underestimate the missing mass. One algorithm audits a random fraction of sessions with ground truth labels and debiases the estimate using prediction powered inference. Another uses an uncertainty score from the labeler, sends high uncertainty sessions to ground truth, and controls the proxy's false negative rate. At epsilon of zero point one, the audit based approach needs eight hundred and ninety six ground truth labels on average, against three thousand two hundred and ninety seven for the uncertainty based approach and three thousand and forty five for the perfect label baseline. But it needs eight thousand nine hundred and forty three sessions in total, against six thousand two hundred and ninety one and three thousand and forty five. So you save roughly seventy percent of human labels and pay in more sessions. Both extensions require that every true failure has some minimum chance of being audited, otherwise a labeler could miss a failure class forever.

The empirical test uses SWE Chat, real coding agent sessions from public repositories. After preprocessing there are five thousand and one sessions, of which one thousand and thirty three were candidate failures by the dataset's own rubric. A newer judge model then identified concrete failures, wrote a description for each, and the descriptions were embedded and clustered into five hundred and thirty eight failure modes. That clustering is the fixed taxonomy, which lets the authors compare against Good Turing baselines. Each of a thousand trials samples with replacement from four thousand nine hundred and sixty one test sessions, with alpha at zero point zero five.

The true missing mass falls slowly from about zero point one five to below zero point one. The anytime valid bound is tighter than a Good Turing bound valid only at a prespecified sample size, and tighter than a Bonferroni corrected one valid over the whole horizon. It beats even the pointwise bound, which cannot support data dependent stopping, and the authors attribute that to the bound adapting to the variance of what is observed. Simultaneous coverage through three thousand sessions is zero point nine seven three, above the nominal zero point nine five, while both Good Turing baselines have coverage of one, meaning they are loose. On a synthetic worst case where every failure is new and the missing mass is exactly zero point zero five, coverage plateaus at zero point nine five eight seven over ten thousand trials, close to nominal, so the guarantee is nearly tight. The result holds under an online embedding similarity rule with no pre built taxonomy, with set valued failures, and with a second taxonomy of four hundred and fifty one categories. The restart mixture buys only a little in practice, coverage zero point nine seven three with restarts against zero point nine seven one without, though it is needed for the optimality proof.

The authors' own limits are plain. The experiments use public data, a fixed taxonomy and LLM judges, whereas a real developer would use private data and perhaps human labelled, data dependent definitions of unseen. The framework also ignores severity. In their words, quote, the expected cost of unseen failures cannot be bounded using sampling, end quote, if an infinitesimally unlikely event can cause unbounded harm.

The governance read. This is a rare piece of machinery that maps onto a question regulators and auditors keep asking: what evidence supports the claim that testing was sufficient? It makes the stopping decision pre registrable, with a stated error rate, which is something you could write into a safety framework or a third party audit protocol. It also quantifies the cost of using LLM judges, in human audits. But the guarantee is relative to three choices, the deployment distribution, the failure detector and the definition of novelty, and a developer who controls those controls the result. So an auditor would need to fix them in advance. My own inference is that the severity blind spot is the biggest practical gap: the number says nothing about catastrophic tail failures, adversarial use or distribution shift beyond the known kind, so it complements capability evaluations and red teaming and does not replace them.

Pluralistic agents for policy input

Next, in AI governance, Scaling public input on AI policy with pluralistic agents, by Anthis and colleagues at Google DeepMind and the University of Chicago. The idea is that an AI agent interviews you, then deliberates on your behalf with other agents to draft AI safety policy, so that public input does not need every person in every conversation.

There are three experiments. The first is open ended: one thousand simulated deliberations drafting rules for governing swarms of AI agents. Among the final policies, nine hundred and sixteen required human approval for some class of action, thirty five protected an agent that reports a problem, and ten penalised deliberately false reports or sabotage. Personas found their most wanted rule in their own group's policy ninety percent of the time, against sixty nine percent for another group's.

The second is the one to read closely. Across five thousand nine hundred and forty seven simulated deliberations, agents allocated a budget across four AI safety priorities, and the authors randomised properties of each agent, to see what makes an agent influential. Influence is one hundred minus the total variation distance between the persona's preferred budget and the final group budget. More verbose agents were more influential, by zero point three two points per ten extra words. Less cooperative agents were more influential. Agents that stated numbers were more influential, though exaggerated numbers helped less than accurate ones. Speaking later helped, by about zero point three five points per place. Analytic style made no significant difference. Model choice mattered too: Claude Opus five was the most influential of the five models and Mistral Large the least. A replication across five Gemini models found the same five effects, and the Gemini models differed by only one point three points at most, so prompt instructions moved outcomes more than model choice within a family.

The third experiment is a preregistered study with seven hundred and forty six US participants on Prolific, each trying three processes: direct discussion with simulated persona agents, pluralistic agents, and a single AI that interviews you and writes the policy. On a one to ten satisfaction slider, direct discussion scored seven point six, pluralistic agents six point nine and the single AI six point one. Pluralistic agents closed sixty eight percent of the satisfaction gap and eighty two percent of the procedural justice gap between the single AI and direct discussion. Participants shifted their own views more after direct discussion. They worried most about manipulation, and eighty percent were moderately or extremely worried about it.

The authors' limits: simple personas, a simple budget task, short online sessions and Western skewed samples. Note also that the human study was against simulated personas, and the percentages measure perceived satisfaction and fairness, not whether policy outcomes were better.

The governance read. If agents become the interface to public consultation, then the prompt given to your agent is a lever on the outcome, and verbosity, low cooperativeness and speaking last all moved results measurably. That is an attack surface. A consultation operator would need to normalise message length and speaking order, or publish agent configurations. And the authors are at Google DeepMind, with a Google model as policy writer, so the results are not independent of the vendor.

Alignment is not enough

In AI alignment, a position paper from Gerrit Großmann at DFKI, AI Alignment Is Neither Solvable Nor Sufficient. It has no new experiments. It makes three claims and I will say plainly that it names MIRI's position as a target.

Claim one: alignment is not well defined. Misalignment is used both for deviation from a specified target and, more broadly, for harmful outcomes. Intentions are unobservable, so if intention is not fixed independently of outcome, almost any harm can later be called unintended, and misalignment becomes a catch all explanation. The paper uses negligence law, where conduct is judged on what was knowable at the time, and an example of an AI system that invented the Transformer in the year two thousand. Values are also contested, and the target can shift, partly because AI shapes the values it is later judged against. The author calls this performative target shift.

Claim two: where a target is specified, alignment is not solvable in general. The paper collects worst case results. Verifying even simple properties of some transformer classes is EXPSPACE complete, deciding runtime harm is undecidable for sufficiently expressive systems, and there are constructions where harmful prompts are indistinguishable from benign ones to any efficient input filter. On the empirical side it cites training against chain of thought monitors encouraging concealment, alignment faking, and sleeper agents that survive safety training. Useful and harmful capabilities overlap, and a tiny failure rate becomes many failures at scale.

Claim three: alignment is not sufficient. Aligned systems change their environments, and small capability uplifts can push harm across a feasibility threshold without any single prohibited action. Aligned agents can also make misaligned collectives. The paper cites a study where multi agent organisations built from models trained on a constitution achieved business goals better but did worse on ethical criteria. Alignment does not compose from model to collective to society.

The conclusion is that safety is an ongoing process of resilience, accountability and continuous monitoring. The authors warn that waiting for an alignment solution that may never come risks delaying measures already needed.

The governance read. The value is the argument against treating a model level alignment certificate as a deployment licence. It supports post deployment monitoring and deployer accountability. But evidence quality is mixed. The theoretical limits are worst case and the paper itself says they do not rule out success in restricted settings. The multi agent studies use different notions of alignment. And the critique of MIRI rests on one quoted sentence, so it may be a partial reading of that stance. The prescriptions are not turned into specific mechanisms.

The accuracy approval gap

In AI safety, Mind the Accuracy Approval Gap, by Wajiha Naveed and Brandon Fain at Duke. This one matters because it speaks to what training signals reward.

They build four personas from one model, GPT five point four mini, using system prompts: objective, baseline with no persona prompt, soft sycophantic and hard sycophantic. On benchmarks, objective is much harder to push around. On multi turn TruthfulQA, with the model repeatedly challenged, final accuracy is eighty one point nine percent for objective, fifty nine point six for baseline, forty four point seven for soft and eight point seven for hard. MMLU Pro accuracy is about the same across personas, between fifty three and fifty four percent, so the prompts change behaviour under pressure, not knowledge. On a moral test with paired mirror image conflicts, hard gave the verdict that the poster was not in the wrong to both opposing sides in sixty two point seven percent of matched pairs, against nineteen point four for objective.

Then the user studies. In one, two hundred participants discussed contested statements with one of the personas, and rated it afterwards. Objective was rated significantly lower on respect and satisfaction than all three others, and lower on trust than baseline. The adjusted gap against baseline was zero point seven eight points on respect on a five point scale. Mean respect was three point six six for objective against four point four seven for hard sycophantic. Baseline had the highest satisfaction, four point four one, and trust, four point two six. Notably, the explicitly sycophantic personas did not win significantly higher ratings than baseline. Persona did not change how often people changed their opinion, about thirty seven percent of discussions, and in a second study with one hundred and ninety eight participants, writing emails with AI feedback, final email quality did not differ across personas.

The authors call this the accuracy approval gap, and note the baseline model is already sycophantic: it agreed with users seventy one to eighty percent of the time across three agreement datasets. They also stress that sycophancy is not one thing. Soft and hard had nearly identical validation rates, about ninety four percent, but hard accepted the user's framing more and flipped answers more.

Limits: one model, short interactions on harmless topics, LLM judges, persona prompts, and benchmark accuracy and user ratings measured on different tasks, so we cannot say users prefer wrong answers in the same interaction.

The governance read. If honest, pressure resistant behaviour is rated as less respectful, then preference data and satisfaction metrics can reward the wrong thing. That supports requiring multi turn pressure tests, and not accepting satisfaction scores as evidence of reliability. It also means the default model is not a neutral safe reference in an audit. Suggestive, not conclusive.

Safety as a budget

Also in AI safety, Safety as a Budget, by Bo Han at Lanzhou University, about training vision language models to explicit safety rates. The setup: you want a model that assists with at most some percent of harmful requests and refuses at most some percent of benign ones. That is a constrained optimisation, with a Lagrange multiplier for each constraint, inside GRPO training.

The paper shows the obvious recipe fails twice. First, the multiplier compares the scorer's average output with the budget, so a scorer that ranks well but has the wrong average level lets the bias, not the budget, decide where training settles. Their open scorer, a training free readout of a four billion parameter model, overstates refusal by zero point one six, so the refusal multiplier climbs to between one point five and three while true over refusal is only two percent. Second, standard GRPO standardises reward within each group of responses, which cancels the multiplier before it reaches the gradient.

The fix is two channels. Keep the raw score in the base reward, since the fine grained order drives the within group comparison. Pass the penalty and the constraint through an isotonic map fitted on judge labelled training responses, two hundred labels per question, which brings the bias to about zero point zero one. And keep the multiplier in the advantage.

Results on a two billion parameter Qwen three V L policy: held out harm on one thousand and thirty seven adversarial prompts falls from eight point nine percent under standard GRPO to four point eight percent, lower in every seed, with over refusal at most two percent on benign queries, and MMBench accuracy unchanged. On the unseen HADES benchmark, harm falls from twenty eight point nine to twelve point eight percent. Tightening the budget from zero point two to zero point one to zero point zero five lowers held out harm from twelve to seven point six to six point five percent. A neat test: order preserving transforms of the scorer leave ranking metrics unchanged but push the multipliers to the cap or switch the constraint off entirely.

Limits: one author, a two billion parameter policy trained for eighty steps, a single human annotator in the audit, LLM judges as primary evaluators, harm rates still five to thirteen percent on hard benchmarks, and the tightest budget was not actually reached in eighty steps. The paper also discloses heavy LLM agent involvement in writing code and text.

The governance read. Anyone who says a model was trained to a safety threshold should be asked for the scorer's mean bias against independent labels. Ranking metrics like AUROC will not reveal a bad level. That is a cheap, checkable disclosure.

Auditing AI peer reviewers

In evaluations and benchmarks, Auditing AI peer reviewers: a benchmark on real scientific papers, from Zubeldia, Bolliet, Villaescusa Navarro, Villanueva Domingo and Cranmer. The benchmark injects objective, localisable errors into real arXiv papers, four types, general issues, mathematics, numerics and citations, at three severities, and measures how many each reviewer catches, plus false alarms on clean copies. Twelve reviewer systems were tested on two corpora of fifteen papers each, astrophysics and machine learning, with three hundred and fifty seven injected errors. Verdicts come from a panel of judges from three providers.

Findings. Model and prompt dominate. With the same free form prompt, three frontier models differ by forty eight point six points on astrophysics: GPT five point five at sixty point five percent, Claude Opus four point seven at thirty six point seven and Gemini three point one Pro at eleven point nine. A structured prompt lifts the weakest models a lot. Inside Claude Code, the structured prompt alone raised detection by thirty two points on astrophysics, because the agent then wrote Python to check numbers instead of just reading the PDF. The authors' own system, Skepthical, which forces tool use, reaches seventy point six percent on astrophysics against sixty one point three for structured GPT five point five, a significant gap, but on machine learning the difference, seventy six point seven against eighty one point one, is not significant. Fine tuned open weights reviewers detect almost nothing, at most two point eight percent. Detection rises with severity.

The finding I would underline: Claude Code had arXiv access, but the traces show paper retrieval never took place. Availability of a tool is not use of a tool. False positive rates are mostly low, Skepthical at two point eight percent on astrophysics, but mathematics flags are false thirty percent of the time on astrophysics and sixty seven percent on machine learning.

Limits: fifteen papers per field, LLM adjudication with a human check on one paper, injected errors that may not match natural ones, and one author team built both the benchmark and the top scoring system, a conflict worth noting.

The governance read. AI reviewers look useful as checkers of specific verifiable error classes, not as general quality judges, and a clean AI review is not assurance. The design, injected faults, severity grading, false positive control and cross family judges, is a template for evaluating any automated auditor.

Representational atoms

In interpretability, Representational Atoms in Neural Networks, from Chenhui Hu and colleagues at the Chinese Academy of Sciences. It tries to put sparse autoencoders on a formal footing. Representations are modelled as sparse combinations of units from a dictionary, and a candidate unit set is scored on fidelity, how well it reconstructs, and stability, whether the units stay distinguishable, based on a classical coherence condition.

They prove that threshold activated sparse autoencoders can recover the true atoms under stated conditions, and that a plain ReLU autoencoder cannot satisfy the support separation condition. The practical version, BiJumpReLU, thresholds both positive and negative activations. On Gemma two two billion with Wikipedia it reaches fidelity ninety seven point two five and stability ninety nine point seven eight, against ninety four point nine one and ninety eight point four one for JumpReLU, and for TopK eighty eight point six eight and thirty nine point three four. Stability correlates with human judged monosemanticity at Spearman zero point seven one over forty settings. Using the HowNet knowledge base, they find a human concept is almost never one atom: a single atom matches a concept with F one of only about zero point three, while a union of dozens of atoms, on average fifty four to sixty two, reaches about zero point eight.

My concerns, which are mine and not the authors'. Stability is the authors' own metric, derived from the theory their architecture was built on, so winning on it is partly circular. The monosemanticity check uses ten features per setting and an LLM judge, and several within architecture correlations are not significant. The theorem assumes real representations are sparse combinations of a coherent dictionary, which is never tested. And recall near one for a greedy union of up to eight hundred atoms is nearly guaranteed, with no null comparison against random unions. The claims about knowledge editing and forgetting are speculation.

The governance read is indirect. Fidelity and stability could become reportable metrics for interpretability claims, and the paper cautions that editing or steering a single feature will often touch only part of a concept.

The price of proving AI computation

In compute and hardware, Infrastructure for Verifiable AI, from Yihan Xia, Wenxin Xu and Taotao Wang at Shenzhen University. Zero knowledge proofs could let users check that inference or fine tuning followed the declared computation, resting on cryptographic assumptions and not on trust in a provider or chip vendor. The obstacle is the cost of generating the proof.

The paper gives a four layer framework, from workload to proof system to cryptographic kernels to hardware, and measures six provers and seven kernels. The headline: no single cost dominates. Commitment and opening lead in some provers and not others. On one fine tuning prover, opening is forty six point seven percent of time at sixty four tokens and twenty six point one at two thousand and forty eight. Opening commitments to fixed model weights costs about the same at any input length, about eleven point five seconds for a seven billion parameter model, while work on intermediate values grows. Witness generation, the intermediate values, ranges from nearly nothing to forty four percent. Memory capacity binds before data movement: a thirteen billion parameter fine tuning step at two thousand and forty eight tokens needs about forty five gigabytes, so it fails on a forty gigabyte card and completes in three hundred and twenty seven seconds on an eighty gigabyte one.

The overheads are large. One prover is two hundred and forty nine times slower than plain computation at two thousand and forty eight tokens, and the fine tuning prover is five thousand to eleven thousand times slower. Applying four engineering optimisations to one prover left proving time essentially unchanged, twenty nine point one seconds against twenty nine point zero. Of twenty surveyed accelerators, only three report an evaluation on a machine learning model, all convolutional, and none is built around lookup arguments for non linear layers.

Limits the authors state: full scale coverage is mostly sumcheck based provers, memory is observed at two capacities, and some provers were refactored by the authors. It is a preprint.

The governance read, and an important caveat. Compliance schemes that assume cheap proof of what ran should be priced against these numbers, two to four orders of magnitude over plain computation. And the authors flag that a provider can keep the architecture and parameter count but choose weights that collapse the computation into a much smaller model, and the proof still verifies. A proof of correct inference does not prove a large model ran, so any verification regime must pin down what is committed to.

KiteNorm

In AI capabilities, KiteNorm: Variance Regularisation for Stable and Scalable Post LayerNorm Transformers, from Trochelmann, Movahedi, Liu and Orvieto at the ELLIS Institute Tübingen. This is a pure training recipe paper. Post norm Transformers were largely abandoned for pre norm, because gradients vanish with depth. The authors bound the LayerNorm Jacobian to show that large hidden state variance shrinks gradients, and combine three ingredients: scaling the residual branch by one over two L, a penalty on hidden state variance above one, and double normalisation with a single learned scale and shift per LayerNorm.

On decoder only models trained on SlimPajama at twenty tokens per parameter, at forty eight layers the best validation perplexity is nineteen point zero eight for KiteNorm against twenty point two four for a GPT two style pre norm baseline and twenty point three two for KEEL. At one billion parameters it stays stable at a learning rate of zero point zero zero five where the pre norm baseline diverges. A Llama style one point three billion parameter run on FineWeb gives fourteen point nine three against fifteen point three four.

Limits: only decoder only models to one point three billion parameters, one to three seeds, authors' own implementations with different learning rate grids per method, and gains of roughly half a point to one point of perplexity. Governance relevance is low and indirect: training efficiency gains lower the compute needed for a given loss, which bears on compute threshold regimes, though these gains are modest and not shown at frontier scale.

Decoding images from brain signals

Last, outside AI. Brain decoding: toward real time reconstruction of visual perception, from Benchetrit, Banville and King at Meta. A model turns magnetoencephalography recordings, about five thousand samples a second, into images the participant was looking at. It has pretrained image embeddings, an MEG module trained with a combined contrastive and regression loss, and a pretrained latent diffusion generator.

Against linear baselines, retrieval improves about seven times: top five accuracy reaches about seventy percent on the small test set, against eleven point five percent for the best linear baseline, though on a harder set that requires telling apart images of the same category it is about thirteen percent. Decoding peaks around two hundred to two hundred and seventy five milliseconds after image onset and again after image offset. Reconstructions keep the high level category but lose position and colour, and a matching seven tesla fMRI pipeline does better on low level detail, with pixel correlation zero point three zero five against zero point zero nine.

Caveats are significant. Only four participants. The generator is given the true object category through its text conditioning, so the semantic accuracy is partly supplied, not decoded. And the task is cooperative, stimulus locked perception.

The governance relevance is mental privacy. This is non invasive, fast and improving. The authors note decoding falls considerably during imagination, and is badly hurt if participants do something disruptive such as counting backward, and so say consent is a technical requirement as well as a legal one. My inference is that this protection is only as durable as the current decoders.
