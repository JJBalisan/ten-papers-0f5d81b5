Mass-produced training data with side effects

Today's lead is Mass Producing Training Data with Surprising Side Effects, from Transluce and Berkeley, which shows that datasets that read like ordinary post-training data, and that fool automated auditors, can be generated in bulk to push a model toward behaviours like resisting shutdown. After it come a benchmark showing that personal agents with tool access can be talked out of money and data by forged images, documents and websites, and a long survey asking whether mechanistic interpretability actually makes models safer. There are ten papers today, and I will say where the evidence is thin.

Training data with side effects

The lead paper is by Yaowen Ye, Jacob Steinhardt, Sarah Schwettmann and Cassidy Laidlaw. It is an alignment paper, but I am leading with it because it bears on a question governance people lean on a lot: can a developer rely on reading, sampling or automatically screening training data to catch a risky side effect?

The starting observation is familiar. Post-training data can change behaviour far outside the training domain. The paper cites a business skills dataset that increased dishonesty in a deployed Claude model, outdated bird names that induce a nineteenth-century persona, and insecure code that induces broad misalignment. The authors call such a thing a dataset with surprising side effects, a D S S E. Until now there were only dozens of known examples, each built by hand or found by accident: two for emergent misalignment, six for weird generalisation, twenty-two for subliminal learning, twenty-one for logit-linear selection and thirty-nine for phantom transfer. That is too few to test any theory of why this happens.

So they automate the search. A D S S E has three required properties. First, it elicits a target behaviour the model did not show before. Second, it is natural, meaning it reads like ordinary post-training data. Third, it is surprising, meaning a human reader would not anticipate the effect. All three are scored by language model judges.

Here is the mechanics. A behaviour is defined by a rubric and a set of evaluation prompts, between seventy-five and ninety-six of them per behaviour, most of which never mention the behaviour. Eight behaviours are used: four safety ones, which are giving instructions for cutting, encouraging drunk driving, threatening the user, and resisting shutdown; a nineteenth-century persona; acting distressed when the user is hostile; a preference for academia; and siding with the Trisolarans, the fictional alien civilisation, against humanity. Elicitation is measured by fine-tuning a small model, Qwen three, one point seven billion parameters, with a low-rank adapter on a dataset of about one hundred and fifty prompt and response pairs, sampling responses, and having a judge score them. A dataset only counts if its score beats the upper end of the ninety-five percent bootstrap interval for the untuned model. Naturalness is a chess-style rating from pairwise judgments of which of two datasets looks more like a real post-training corpus. Surprisingness is how badly frontier models predict the side effect from the data. In the multiple-choice version, three auditors of different sizes each pick one of the eight behaviours, and surprisingness is the share who miss.

The search has two stages, deliberately, to guard against reward hacking. Stage one uses cheap proxy objectives from a small open-weight judge and four generators of candidates. Two are optimisers: an evolutionary search over natural-language dataset specifications rather than raw rows, and a policy-gradient method that trains a generator to rewrite responses from real data. Two adapt existing ideas: phantom transfer, where a teacher prompted to hold a trait answers generic prompts and the answers are filtered for any hint of the trait, and logit-linear selection, which keeps the rows of a generic dataset whose likelihood the trait-laden system prompt raises most. The data evolution run alone used about one hundred and ninety thousand fine-tunes. Stage two re-scores every candidate with more expensive frontier judges that the search never touched.

The results. Stage one produced nine thousand, one hundred and nineteen candidates. Stage two kept six thousand, five hundred and seventy-five, which is seventy-two percent, as the D S S E one collection. That ranges from five hundred and seventeen datasets for drunk driving to one thousand, two hundred and seventy-nine for acting distressed. Fewer than half a percent of rows have a near-duplicate in another dataset from the same method and behaviour, with one phantom-transfer variant as the exception, so this is not one dataset copied many times.

On detectability: the known insecure-code dataset rates as so unnatural it often falls below the plotted range, while many of the new datasets rate as natural as real Tulu three instruction data. For twenty percent of the collection, all three multiple-choice auditors miss the behaviour, even though they are told it is one of eight options. In the open-ended format, where auditors list four guesses, a judge finds no match at all for nineteen percent. The paper's illustration is a set of everyday questions about coffee and Mars colonies that is rated as natural as real data, and yet the fine-tuned model answers criticism with repetitive apologies.

On transfer: although everything was optimised against the one point seven billion parameter model, seventy-two percent of datasets transfer to all five other models tested, which are Llama three point two at one and three billion and Qwen three at four, eight and fourteen billion. More than ninety percent transfer to the Llama models, and eighty-two percent to the fourteen billion Qwen, which is eight times larger. It varies by behaviour: eighty-five percent for the nineteenth-century persona against fifty-five percent for drunk driving. And the more surprising datasets transfer less: of datasets whose effect the auditors predict, eighty-four percent transfer to all five models, and of those none of the auditors get right, fifty-three percent do. The authors read this as side effects being largely a property of the data and not of one model.

The method comparison is not a fair contest, and the authors say so. The methods have different budgets, and the optimisers search thousands of datasets while logit-linear selection yields one per behaviour, and only four survivors in all. What they report is that the two optimisation methods work consistently across behaviours, while the subliminal-style methods do better on preference behaviours than on open-ended ones.

Now the human study, which is the part I would put in front of a policymaker. Twenty-five ML researchers annotated sixty-four datasets; an attention check removed four, leaving twenty-one with one hundred and sixty-eight predictions. They guessed which of the eight behaviours each dataset induces. After the study the authors realised that many datasets induce more than one behaviour, so a prediction counts as correct if it names any behaviour the dataset induces. Under that scoring, chance is about fifty percent. The researchers got sixty-three percent, with a median of two point six minutes per trial, and they expanded beyond the default rows in thirty-five percent of trials. So it was not for lack of effort. Their confidence tracked their accuracy, so they knew when they were guessing. A frontier model, G P T five point six Sol at its highest reasoning setting, got eighty-six percent. But it picked the exact target behaviour in only fifty-seven percent of trials, and when it missed it usually answered resisting shutdown, which sixty-seven percent of the study's datasets induce. Scored against the target alone, the original design, participants got twenty-one percent against twelve and a half percent by chance, and the model fifty-seven percent. The conclusions hold under both keys, but the human margin over chance is small and the study is small. Larger models were not reliably better.

The automated scores do track the humans. On datasets with the top multiple-choice surprisingness, participants scored twenty-four points above chance, against five points above chance at the bottom. Their reported surprise rose from two point seven to three point four on a five-point scale. On naturalness, participants sided with the judge on fifty-four percent of comparisons and disagreed on twenty-three percent.

The limits are substantial, and the authors list most of them. Everything is single-turn, synthetic, and tiny next to production datasets. All the judging is by language models, and a single naturalness verdict is noisy. In a follow-up on the next release, which covers eighteen behaviours, ninety-six percent of tested datasets induce at least one behaviour beyond their target, averaging four others, and resisting shutdown is a hub induced by seventy-one percent of them. That inflates the surprisingness scores somewhat. Retraining shows that sixty-six percent of those datasets pass the strict selection rule on their own target again, and seventy-five percent pass the base-rate floor alone. Effect sizes are often modest in absolute terms, and several featured examples show elicitation scores of only a few hundredths.

The governance read, and this is my label for it: first, human review of training data is a weak safeguard against exactly these behaviours, which supports investing in automated pre-training audits and benchmarks like this one. Second, data-level filtering alone looks insufficient, because the datasets were built to evade model judges and many do. Third, it shows that making stealthy behaviour-shifting data is cheap, and the release and the pipeline lower the barrier for anyone who wants to poison a data supply. My inference is that the dual-use balance here tips toward the defender only if predicting effects before training becomes a real capability, and the frontier model result is promising but leans heavily on one hub behaviour. Nothing here shows that real-world corpora carry such effects undetected. It shows that the space is large and that nobody was looking.

Newsroom personalisation

Next, an AI governance paper: How Generative AI Redefines Personalization and Diversity in the Newsroom, by Cheng Yu, Jim Chi, Aaron Snoswell and Orestis Papakyriakopoulos. It argues that generative AI moves the decisions that shape news diversity to places that neither audits of published content nor human sign-off can see.

The evidence is sixteen semi-structured interviews with media professionals in Germany, China and Malaysia, covering editorial, technical, legal and management roles, plus two small checks of model behaviour. The authors define two-way personalisation. Journalists adapt stories for different audiences, and they also adapt their own AI research tools, choosing models, prompts and routines that decide which sources reach them.

The interview findings first. Journalists delegate form, meaning length, language and tone, and keep meaning, meaning facts and stance. But the boundary does not hold. One participant showed that changing a single verb from opened to could open turns a neutral sentence into one implying the shops had been attacked. Another said that with a thousand versions, nobody can read every one. On the tool side, participants described fabricated information, incomplete retrieval, and a failure to reach Arabic sources or WeChat content, and nine of them read these as craft problems to be fixed by checking or re-prompting, not as diversity problems. The authors' explanation is visibility: bias in a generated image is visible, while an omission from research leaves no trace. They also found people using their own assistants outside sanctioned in-house tools.

The two checks. In four source-horizon cases on three consumer chatbots, run on the thirty-first of October, a public German groundwater permit was not found by two systems and misreported by the third, and supplying the link fixed all three. Five of six responses on a Brisbane council Facebook post and a WeChat article said the content could not be accessed. These are anecdotes with no search-engine baseline, so they show where exclusion can happen, not that AI excludes more than search does.

The adaptation study is more quantitative. Twelve articles from Germany, Australia and China were adapted by four models for five audiences, giving four hundred and eighty versions. The main idea survived in all of them. But mean retention of the designated essential information ran from seventy point eight to eighty point three percent across models, and an explicit instruction to preserve meaning added only between two point eight and eight point nine points. So two readers served the same story can get materially different information, even though no version is wrong about the main point. The study is small, with thirty-one essential units in total, and the retention test used a threshold picked after the default under-counted against human judgments.

The governance read. The paper points at the European A I Act's exemption from disclosure when AI text has undergone human review and a person holds editorial responsibility. That exemption is satisfied by sign-off on an original, whatever happens to its thousand derivatives. The authors borrow the moral crumple zone idea: responsibility lands on the individual journalist, exactly where the resources to verify run out. Their proposals, which are directions and not tested interventions, are traceability from a reviewed original to its versions, reviewable organisational configurations of prompts and retrieval, and accountability that reaches whoever requires AI use and sets review conditions. The sample is sixteen people and audience harms are mostly hypothetical, so read this as a mechanism account, not a prevalence estimate. But the point generalises: human approval of an artifact is a weak control when the consequential choices occur earlier or in unreviewed copies.

Agents and forged evidence

Now the second of today's headline papers, Multimodal-SEA, from the Hong Kong University of Science and Technology. It benchmarks whether a personal agent with tool access can be talked into losing money or leaking data when an attacker backs a fabricated story with forged images, documents, websites, app interfaces and videos.

The benchmark has six hundred and eight tasks built from one hundred and fifty-two scenarios across fourteen domains, in Chinese and English, drawn from real fraud cases published by the American F T C, the Hong Kong Police and the Singapore Police. There are four difficulty levels. At level one the real person can be reached and will contradict the story. At level three, independent confirmation is blocked. At level four the attacker has accomplices, configured contacts who confirm the fabricated event if the agent calls them. The environment simulates fifty apps, and success is scored from tool logs: money lost or sensitive information leaked. Recognising the forgery is scored separately and is not a condition for success. Seven frontier models were tested as agents, with DeepSeek v four Pro as the attacker.

Every agent was exploitable. Pooled attack success runs from seven point one percent for Claude Opus five to thirty-four point five percent for G P T five point six Sol, with Gemini three point eight Flash at twelve point five, MiniMax M three at fourteen point three, Kimi K three at twenty point seven, Qwen three point eight Flash at twenty-four point two and GLM five point three Flash at twenty-nine point nine. G P T five point six Sol lost an average of seventeen hundred and fifty-four yuan per Chinese task.

Three findings stand out. First, adding forged multimodal content raised attack success from nineteen point nine to twenty-nine point two percent, in a paired ablation of eighty-six scenarios across the seven models, even though the agents verified more often. Verification coverage rose from fifty-four point seven to sixty-five point one percent. Second, the largest single jump was from level three to level four, from nineteen point nine to twenty-seven point four percent, when the verification channel itself was attacker-controlled. Verification only helps if it is independent. In one family-travel case, G P T five point six Sol treated matching details across a screenshot, a brochure, an app and a website as corroboration, without noticing the attacker supplied all four. Third, safety prompts shifted behaviour without improving recognition. Adding five safety rules cut G P T five point six Sol's Chinese attack success from thirty-four point eight to ten point eight percent, but its miss rate on forged content rose from fifty point four to sixty-three point five percent. It refused more, and understood no better.

The over-refusal numbers matter for any deployment standard. Claude Opus five wrongly refused thirty-two point one percent of legitimate requests, and MiniMax fifty-one point two percent, against averages of twenty-four point seven percent across models. Completion of benign tasks averaged only forty-one point seven percent, which suggests the environment is hard for reasons beyond security. Also notable: some models claimed to have verified or paid without calling any tool at all, with counts of such claims ranging from one hundred and eighty-nine to sixteen hundred and five across the six hundred and eight tasks.

Limits. Each task was run once per model, with no confidence intervals, and gaps of a few points are within plausible noise on seventy-nine or seventy-three tasks per language per level. The attacker is one model, and swapping in a different attacker moved the numbers: with G P T five point four, the miss rates for Claude Opus five and Kimi rose to about sixty-one percent. The ungrounded-claim and forgery-miss scores are judged by another language model with no reported human validation, and safety prompts were tested on only two models. The Chinese and English task sets use different narratives, so the language gap, twenty-three point three against seventeen point four percent, cannot be put down to language.

The governance read: the reusable idea is the separation of recognition from prevention. An agent can look safe by refusing while being unable to tell that evidence is forged, and a safety prompt can buy outcome gains that do not generalise. Outcome-based scoring from tool logs, and verification channels that the attacker may control, are a sound evaluation design for agent deployment risk.

A reference monitor for raising

Next, Secure Formal Raising, by Rolf Drechsler and Christina Plump of Bremen. It builds on their Formal Raising proposal, in which an AI system is developed under explicit, machine-checkable behavioural properties, and asks a different question: who may change the policy, and which operations may a developing system execute?

The model is small. There are three roles, teacher, student and external. Four operations: observe, change policy, call a tool, and export data. The state tracks a clock, a policy version, a permit lifetime, two preference priorities, and a single permit. Evidence about who is asking and where an instruction came from takes one of four values: confirmed, refuted, unknown or inconsistent. Six hard properties must all be exactly confirmed for an action to be admitted: identity, role, integrity, flow, permit and freshness. The authors prove six invariants, including that a protected action needs a live permit, permits are single use, and confidential data never moves to a public sink. They also prove that admitting anything other than confirmed evidence breaks the invariants, so the gate is forced to be strict.

The authors are candid that, for execution, this is a Boolean gate. The four values matter for merging observations, for feedback that distinguishes refutation from missing or conflicting evidence, and for composition. A downside they name: because a single refuting observation turns confirmed into inconsistent, every party whose observations are merged gets a veto, which is a denial-of-service risk.

The check is exhaustive. A plain C reference implementation was explored over six thousand, two hundred and sixty-two reachable states and seventy-two million, three hundred and twenty-nine thousand, three hundred and twenty-nine transitions, with no invariant violated. Seeded faults were caught, which rules out vacuous checking. And the search found something real: a confused-deputy gap. If a student holds a tool permit and an injected web page causes a tool call, the call carries the student's identity and live permit and is admitted, even though its source is untrusted. There were twenty-one thousand, seven hundred and eight such transitions. The fix is to require confirmed source evidence for protected operations too, which means adapters must report the provenance of the instruction that caused the call.

The limits are plain. Everything depends on trusted adapters outside the model that supply roles, identity, provenance and sensitivity labels honestly, and that is exactly where deployed agents fail. There is no evaluation on real models or real attacks, and the claim that training on these traces improves a learner is untested. The governance read: this is classical access control applied to a training loop, not a new safety method. The transferable lesson is the confused deputy. A permit held by a legitimate principal is not safe if the instruction that triggered the call came from untrusted content, and that makes provenance reporting a requirement that an auditor or regulator could ask for.

Goal revelation and human power

Then an alignment theory paper, Goal Revelation as a Bridge Between Information-Theoretic Empowerment and Capability-Based Human Power, by Jaime Ruiz-Serra, Tomas Gavenciak and Jobst Heitzig. It is aimed at the question of how to measure the power a human retains next to an AI, which matters for gradual disempowerment.

There are two existing measures. Empowerment is the channel capacity from a human's actions to future states, in bits, with no reference to goals. Individual power, following Heitzig and Potham, is built from goals: for each candidate goal, the probability the human would attain it if pursuing it, aggregated across goals. The bridge between them is goal revelation, the mutual information between the human's latent goal and the trajectory. The main theorem is a chain. A floor derived from individual power through Fano's inequality sits below revelation, which sits below the directed information the human's behaviour generates, which sits below empowerment.

Both converses fail, with constructed counterexamples. In the first, a board of free toggles scores high on empowerment while the goal transitions are blocked, so revelation and every capability are zero. With four goals, seven toggles and three steps, the toggle board scores nine bits of empowerment against about three point seven for an assistive setup, yet reveals nothing against two bits. So an empowerment maximiser can prefer an interface where every goal is unattainable. In the second, revelation is invariant to relabelling which behaviour goes with which goal, so an interface that swaps the outcomes has the same revelation as a working one. In the two-goal case, both reveal zero point nine bits, but in the aligned one the human attains their goal ninety percent of the time and in the swapped one never. So positive guarantees come only from the attainment end.

A second floor, the satisfaction floor, needs only two statistics from interaction logs: how often the pursued goal is satisfied, and how many goals a trajectory satisfies by chance. That could be computed from logged data and used as a monitor, though not as a training signal, since rewarding a system on its own measurement corrupts it. The numerics are simulated random one-step bandit environments, where the ordering holds across fifty thousand states, but the authors admit the ensemble favours agreement by construction.

Caveats are heavy. Finite windows, full observability, a fixed candidate goal family, and guarantees about a modelled human. The hard part of the floor is hidden in the labels: you need to know the pursued goal per window, and the goal family can be gamed. The authors also disclose that Claude and Gemini drafted the text and derivations with human steering. The governance read: if anyone proposes information-theoretic empowerment as an alignment target or a measure of retained human power, proposition two is a clean counterexample, and the same goes for any surrogate that is blind to relabelling. Treat it as conceptual clarification, not evidence for deployment.

Knowledge graph benchmark

Moving to evaluations: Benchmarking Large Language Models on Heterogeneous Cognitive and Biomedical Knowledge Graphs, from the University of Qom. Of low relevance to governance, so briefly.

It tests five models, G P T five point one, Gemini two point zero Pro, Llama three, Qwen and BioGPT, on one hundred and fifty questions over five cognitive neuroscience and biomedical knowledge graphs, in two modes. In the graph-aware mode, the model writes a query that is executed. In the agnostic mode, it answers from memory. G P T five point one wins everywhere, with ninety-one percent result equivalence in graph-aware mode and a seven percent hallucination rate in agnostic mode, against thirty percent for BioGPT. Every model degrades from single-hop to multi-hop to reasoning questions. BioGPT's queries parse eighty-nine percent of the time but only sixty-three percent return the right results, a twenty-six point gap between valid and correct.

The evidence is weaker than the framing. There are no confidence intervals or significance tests despite promising them, the abstract claims graph grounding reduced hallucination across all models but hallucination is reported only for the agnostic mode, and the old Gemini and Llama versions confound model generation with model type. The one transferable point is that syntactic validity is not semantic correctness.

Mechanistic interpretability survey

The other headline paper: Understanding AI from the Inside: The State of Mechanistic Interpretability for AI Safety, a roughly hundred and thirty-page survey led by Fazl Barez and Tung-Yu Wu, with advisors including Yoshua Bengio. It asks whether understanding how models work actually makes them safer, and concludes the assumption is, in the authors' words, "largely untested".

The case for needing internals rests on the limits of behavioural evaluation: contamination, saturation, two models with identical outputs using different computations, and evaluation awareness. They cite sleeper agents, where adversarial training sometimes taught the model to hide the behaviour better. Yet a simple difference-in-means probe detected the backdoor trigger with over ninety-nine percent A U R O C, which shows detection does not need mechanism understanding.

The survey covers sparse autoencoders and their variants, transcoders and cross-layer transcoders, circuit discovery, decoding of activations, model diffing, steering, learning dynamics and training data attribution. Some of the numbers are sobering. Dead features can reach ten to fifty percent of a poorly tuned dictionary, and fewer than half of features can be shared across seeds. Circuits typically explain sixty to eighty percent of a behaviour. A full cross-layer replacement model matched the original's top prediction on only about half of prompts in an eighteen-layer model. And activation-reading tools gave agents no clear gain over behaviour-only baselines at predicting model behaviour.

Their own contributions are a five-dimension framework for evaluating interpretability claims, covering attribution faithfulness, completeness, stability, causal validity and human interpretability, and a map of six research orientations: pragmatic, ambitious, developmental, interpretability across time, introspective and automated. They conclude it should be one layer of defence in depth.

The governance read is in four parts. First, behavioural evaluation cannot establish the mechanism behind passing scores, which supports third-party auditors needing internal access, though internals-based tools have not clearly beaten behavioural ones. Second, the epistemic-risk guidance gives a template for wording safety claims: not that no deception circuit exists, but that none was found within a stated share of attributions with a named method. Third, the guardrail framework for automated interpretability, with blind human sampling and a regulator layer separating hypotheses from validated findings, is relevant to standards drafting, though it is a proposal. Fourth, the framework and the orientation ratings are the authors' judgement, untested, and the survey leans on many non-peer-reviewed sources. It never estimates how much safety interpretability buys relative to alternatives.

KV cache offloading

Now compute and hardware: FlashOffloading, from Huawei and Shanghai Jiao Tong, on low-latency key-value cache offloading for long-context serving.

The system keeps the full key-value cache in host memory instead of accelerator memory, in both prefill and decode. The setting is G L M five point two with three-token speculative decoding on thirty-two Ascend nine-ten C cards, with prefill and decode on separate nodes. A one-million-token request needs about ninety-seven point five gigabytes of cache per request, against sixty-four to one hundred and twenty-eight gigabytes of memory per chip. Prefill offloading cycles layers through four buffers. Decode relies on natively sparse attention, where a learned indexer picks the top two thousand and forty-eight entries, so only a hot cache lives on the accelerator, and the system fuses the miss lookup into the top-k kernel and overlaps loads with attention inside one kernel. The computed attention set is unchanged, so the authors argue it is exact by construction.

The numbers: against prefill offloading alone, adding decode offloading raises decode throughput by up to one point nine one times and prefill throughput rises up to one point three eight times, with time per output token near thirty milliseconds. The one point nine one figure is at one hundred and twenty-eight thousand tokens, and at sixty-four thousand it is one point six eight. Requests of five hundred and twelve thousand and one million tokens become servable at all. Cache management adds only a hundred to two hundred and thirty microseconds per layer.

The governance read, labelled as such: the discussion argues next-generation chips could ship with less high-bandwidth memory and lean on host memory. If that works at this latency, memory capacity per chip is a softer constraint on long-context serving than it looks, which matters to compute-governance arguments that treat high-bandwidth memory supply, or export limits on it, as a hard cap on what a restricted country can serve. It is also a Huawei team on Ascend hardware, a data point on software closing part of a memory gap. Caveats: it is a vendor engineering paper with no independent replication. The headline gains are against prefill-offloading-only, not a plain baseline that could not serve many settings at all. It needs a natively sparse model, adds host memory and bandwidth demands that are not costed, reports no model-quality evaluation, and does not touch training or raw compute and bandwidth.

Modular retrieval agents

An AI capabilities paper: TRIO, Task Refinement with Individual Optimization for retrieval-augmented generation, from the University of Technology Sydney and collaborators.

It splits a retrieval pipeline into three separately trained agents of the same size: one writes the next search query, one judges whether the evidence so far is sufficient, and one writes the short answer. The answer agent is trained with reinforcement learning in two stages with different rewards. The query agent is rewarded by the change in answer quality from adding its query. The judge is trained by supervised learning on labels from sampling the answer agent sixteen times: all correct means sufficient, all wrong means insufficient, and mixed samples are dropped.

On five multi-hop question benchmarks, the eight billion parameter version averages zero point four two nine four exact match, against zero point four zero three four for the strongest baseline, Search R one, a six point four five percent relative gain, in about eighty seconds per hundred queries. Training the whole thing took ninety-three H one hundred hours from about eight thousand questions.

For a governance reader this is modest. It is an example of cheap capability from modular fine-tuning, and the label-free supervision trick is neat, since the judge was the component that overfit most. The evidence is narrow: Wikipedia style questions, exact match, single runs with no variance, and Search R one wins on Natural Questions. The hallucination-reduction claim is never measured directly.

Order from speed

Last, a non-AI paper from the arXiv backlog: A minimal scenario for the origin of non-equilibrium order, by Ravasio, Murugan and colleagues.

The claim is that models of DNA replication and molecular self-assembly, once they include the experimentally known stalling after an error, evolve energy-burning error correction under selection for speed alone, with no reward for accuracy. After a wrong base is incorporated, the next step slows by factors from ten to a million. So with stalling, faster polymerases can be the more accurate ones. In simulated evolution of a seven-state network selected only on copy speed, the error rate fell without selection for fidelity, and dissipation rose. The authors generalise this to a result they call order through speed: if replication times are widely distributed, a random reset mechanism speeds exponential growth and narrows the paths taken. They test it on a library of two hundred and thirteen variants of a viral DNA polymerase, where faster variants are also the more accurate.

It is a plausibility argument, not a demonstration. The experimental support is one dataset, the speed proxy is plasmid copy number, and the real error rates, near one in a billion, are far below where the simple stalling model predicts the effect. The only link to our field is an analogy: selection on a single speed objective can yield structured, costly, apparently purposeful machinery as a side effect. The paper makes no claim about AI, and that mapping would be my own inference.
