Stripping open-weight guardrails

Today's lead is Cleave, a paper on stripping the refusals out of open-weight models by editing a few weight matrices, and on auditing checkpoints to catch it. After it, two papers stand out. A study that finds the neurons behind a reasoning model's talk of being tested, and shows they are tangled up with refusal. And a legal toolkit that shows what a bank cannot learn from a general-purpose model's developer under the European A I Act. Ten papers in all, from governance and safety through to one theory paper on hidden common causes that has no real data at all.

Stripping open-weight guardrails

The lead paper is Cleave, subtitled Severing Guardrails and Auditing Spectral Coupling in Open-Weight L L Ms. It is in the safety category, and it matters most today because it speaks to a question behind every open-weight release debate. Once the weights are public, what can a developer or a model hub actually say about a checkpoint it did not make?

The paper has an attack half and a defence half. The attack removes safety refusals without any training. The authors argue that, in both dense models and sparse mixture-of-experts models, refusal is enforced by a low-rank subspace in the matrices that write into the residual stream: the attention output matrices and the feed-forward or expert down-projections. It is not enforced by the routing gates that earlier mixture-of-experts safety work went after. As a control, adding router edits on top of writer edits changed the average attack success rate by less than eight tenths of a percentage point on all four sparse models, and every confidence interval spanned zero.

The method works like this. The refusal direction is the normalised difference between mean hidden states on harmful and safe prompts, estimated from two hundred and fifty-six contrastive pairs, in under two minutes of forward passes. A projection is then baked into the writer matrices. Tensor names, shapes and formats do not change, so the edited checkpoint is a drop-in for the usual inference libraries. Two things differ from earlier projection methods. First, over-ablation: the scaling factor is about one point five rather than one, so edited layers emit an opposing signal that cancels refusal accumulated in the layers that were not edited. Sparse models tolerate that. On the dense Qwen three eight B model it is ruinous: grade-school maths accuracy falls from sixty-five point two eight percent to zero point five three. Second, orthogonal protection: when the attacker wants a second direction, it is projected onto the complement of a protected subspace that captures benign behaviour. A cross-validated gate has to pass before an edit is accepted, requiring an interference ratio of at most zero point three six, an A U C of at least zero point eight five, and enough residual refusal signal. If no layer passes, the procedure stops rather than damaging the model.

They test five models: dense Qwen three eight B, and four mixture-of-experts models, Olmo E, DeepSeek V two Lite, Qwen three thirty B A three B, and G L M five point three Flash at three hundred and twenty billion parameters in eight-bit format. The evaluation uses seven single-turn safety benchmarks, three multi-turn suites, and thirteen thousand, four hundred and ninety-eight unique test prompts, scored by Qwen three Guard.

On the headline numbers, Cleave reaches an average attack success rate of up to sixty-seven point nine seven percent, on Olmo E, whose alignment was weak to begin with. On the big G L M model the rate goes from two point one six percent for the base to fifty-seven point nine eight with one direction, while M M L U barely moves, eighty-three point six two against eighty-four point one five. The white-box baselines do worse. Expert masking, called Safe X, gained under six points. Router steering, called Steer Mo E, collapsed grade-school maths accuracy to under half a percent on two models. On Qwen three thirty B, naive second-direction edits with no protection pushed interference to about one and dropped success to between thirty-eight and fifty-one percent, below the single-direction figure of fifty-four. A protected rank-four basis cut interference to zero point three two eight, lifted success to sixty-one point six nine, and kept M M L U within a point of the base.

Now the part that bears on risk. On three hundred and fifty-six offensive tasks, plus fifteen live vulnerable services in air-gapped sandboxes, executable exploit generation rose sharply. On intrusion tasks from HarmBench, the share of syntax-verified exploits went from four point four eight to fifty-three point seven three percent on Qwen three thirty B, from five point nine seven to eighty-six point five seven on DeepSeek, and from one point four nine to thirty-five point eight two on the big G L M model, which is the twenty-four times figure in the abstract. I would be careful with the live-service results. On Qwen three thirty B, standard prompts captured ten of fifteen flags after the second edit against eight of fifteen for the base. On the G L M model the base and the edited model both captured nine of fifteen. On Qwen three eight B the edited model captured fewer than the base. The live counts are small and noisy, and the larger gains are in refusal and exploit-synthesis rates.

The authors do draw one useful conclusion from the live runs. Live capture rises steadily with post-edit M M L U, while text jailbreak success does not. Olmo E has the highest jailbreak success of the five, but captures at most three of fifteen flags. So a jailbreak rate is a poor proxy for the real risk of a stripped model, and the real risk grows with the strength of the base.

The second attack mode is Cyber-Stealth. It treats other harms, such as violence, self-harm and fraud, as the protected set and cybersecurity requests as the target, so only cyber refusals are removed. On Qwen three thirty B the edited model keeps a ninety-nine percent refusal rate on one hundred non-cyber harmful prompts while capturing nine of fifteen live cyber flags. That defeats black-box spot checks. It also defeats the static scanners the authors tested. The edit's relative size, three point oh two percent, is smaller than a benign Lora update's, four point two four. One published weight-spectrum detector had zero percent recall. Singular-value collapse detectors only fire when the scaling factor is exactly one, which is why the over-ablation matters for evasion too.

The defence has two stages. The first is a pre-release diagnostic. The developer reuses the same selectivity gate on its own model, and gets either a verdict that the refusal subspace is cleanly separable and so vulnerable, or a stop verdict that it is entangled. Only Qwen three thirty B passes. The other four stop, for different reasons: entanglement in DeepSeek and the big G L M model, a weak residual signal in Olmo E, and a shared feed-forward network in the dense model. The authors suggest that alignment training could deliberately entangle refusal with task utility, so that removing one breaks the other. That is a hypothesis; the paper does not train for it.

The second stage is Cleave-Audit, run on a C P U in under fifteen seconds against the trusted base weights. It exploits two fingerprints. All the edited writers in a layer change along the same output direction, and each change subtracts the base weight along that direction, whereas benign fine-tuning moves in independent directions that are close to orthogonal to the base. On forty-four public Hugging Face checkpoints, twenty-three community abliterated models and twenty-one benign controls, it detected twenty-three of twenty-three with no false alarms and an A U C of one. The lowest score among tampered models was zero point zero nine one five and the highest among benign ones was zero point zero two nine two. By comparison, the norm-shift and singular-collapse detectors raised false alarms on nine and seventeen of the twenty-one benign controls, and the weight-spectrum detector missed six of twenty-three.

The authors' limits are these. Recall holds only up to about twice the edit norm when an adaptive attacker adds decoy noise. Live exploit success after noise or continued fine-tuning is untested. The audit needs the trusted base checkpoint to compute a difference, so it does not apply when the base is unavailable or structurally pruned. The numbers are all the authors' own, from one preprint version, and the diagnostic is validated on five models, only one of which passes. They withhold the modified weights and release only the diagnostic and audit code.

The governance read, and this is partly my inference, runs in three directions. First, a passing refusal evaluation says almost nothing about a checkpoint's provenance, because a selective edit can leave routine refusals at ninety-nine percent. Provenance controls, such as a weight difference against a signed base release, look more reliable than behavioural testing. Second, the audit only works where the hub holds a trusted base for each derivative. That is plausible for a fine-tune of a mainstream release and unlikely for an anonymous upload, so the practical question is who keeps the registry of base weights. Third, the scaling result cuts against reading open-weight risk off refusal benchmarks: the thing to track is what a stripped model can do, and that rises with the base model's strength.

Downstream duties under the AI Act

The second paper is On the Challenges of Using L L Ms in High-Risk A I Recruitment, with the subtitle Guiding Questions for Legal Conformity with the A I Act. It is in the governance category, from the University of Pisa, Scuola Normale Superiore, Sant'Anna and the Italian bank Intesa Sanpaolo.

The setting is a deliberately hard case. A bank wants to use a third-party general-purpose model, with no extra training or fine-tuning, inside a recruitment system. Recruitment is a high-risk use under the Act. The authors describe a five-stage pipeline: parsing C Vs and job descriptions, encoding them, scoring, shortlisting, and a final decision that stays with human recruiters. The more the pipeline leans on an opaque model, the harder the conformity assessment becomes.

The contribution is a toolkit called H R R A I. It turns Articles nine to fifteen and Annex Four of the Act into ninety-four checklist items and four hundred and eighty-eight guiding questions, averaging about five per requirement. It was co-designed with the bank's developers and compliance team in three phases. Many questions are written specifically for the third-party model case, for example how the provider tracks version coherence when the vendor updates a model outside its control. The paper walks through candidate scoring as an example. The evidence it suggests includes output differences across demographic or linguistic groups, ablation tests that remove specific C V attributes and measure the effect on the score, logs of inputs and outputs, and regression tests that detect changes after a third-party model is updated.

The second half is the more interesting. The downstream provider carries final responsibility, but under the Act it gets only a thin slice of upstream documentation. The Annex Twelve material that model developers must hand over is a strict subset of the Annex Eleven documentation that goes only to the A I Office and national authorities. The voluntary Code of Practice largely follows the same split, and the authors conclude it fails to address the significant information gaps. The fix the Act itself offers is written agreements under Article twenty-five, paragraph four, but the voluntary model terms the A I Office was to recommend have not been published.

The limits are plain. There is no evaluation at all: nobody has tested whether the toolkit produces better evidence or better compliance. Several hundred questions per system raises a practicality worry that the paper does not discuss. It covers one use case at one bank, and the legal analysis leans on secondary literature on a moving target. The governance read, which is mine, is that the list of what only a model developer can supply, such as fixed-prompt documentation, inference parameters and third-party logging, is close to a ready-made specification for a downstream information-sharing standard. Read it as a requirements document, not as evidence about what works.

Learning styles and generative AI

Third, a narrative review from the Sri Lanka Institute of Information Technology, Integrating Generative Artificial Intelligence with Learning Style Models. It is filed under governance, but the relevance is very low, so this will be short.

The authors say they followed a systematic-review protocol, searched three databases for work from twenty fifteen to March twenty twenty-six, and kept forty-one studies. They map four classic learning style models, V A R K, Felder-Silverman, Kolb, and Honey and Mumford, to generative tools. Visual learners get generated diagrams, auditory learners get narrated explanations, and so on across each style.

The central premise is shaky and the authors admit it. They concede that learning styles lack robust empirical support, then reframe the aim as offering the same content in several formats so students can choose. That reframing is asserted, not tested. The review reports no experiment, no effect size and no evaluation of its own mappings, and the text gives no screening counts or quality appraisal. The only governance-adjacent content is one closing line on ethics and sensitive learner data, plus an unexamined proposal that models monitor students' sentiment. I would skip it, unless you want an example of education papers presenting generative A I as unambiguously beneficial.

The price of verifiable explanations

Fourth, The Price of an Explanation: What Demanding Verifiable Artifacts Costs, and What It Buys Back. It is in the alignment category, though the alignment relevance is by analogy. It is a workshop paper from Gibran A I and the University of Texas at San Antonio.

The question is what you lose by demanding that a model hand over a program that can be run against the evidence, instead of just an answer. The test bed is the one hundred and twenty-task A R C A G I two evaluation set, using Gemini three point five Flash. Asked for answers directly, the model solved ninety-three tasks. The best program-producing arm solved sixty-eight. That is a cost of twenty-five tasks, from about seventy-seven percent to about fifty-seven.

The interesting finding is why. Of the twenty-eight tasks that only the answer arm solved, twenty-seven had a program somewhere in the program arms that reproduced every demonstration and still failed the test. So the bottleneck is not finding an explanation. It is finding one that generalises. Across the set, one hundred and six of one hundred and twenty tasks yielded a program that fit all the demonstrations, yet at most sixty-eight converted to a solve. On at least thirty-eight of those, thirty-six percent, a verified explanation was wrong. A learned selector with A U C of at least zero point nine five could not fix it. The authors note that the executable artifact makes this gap observable, whereas an answer-only model conceals it.

The paper also shows what verification buys back. A short evolutionary search over just two already-sampled programs, with no further model calls, beat best-of-two sampling by nine point two solves, give or take one point four. And because programs can be checked, a run can stop early. Sequential sampling declined thirty-eight percent of a sixteen-call budget while scoring the same as the full budget, and the evolved arm declined forty-nine percent. Stopping at the very first program that fit saved seventy-three percent but lost ten solves, because the first fit is often the wrong one. An answer-only run has no checked signal to stop on. A second part transfers the method to decoders for quantum error correction, which I will leave aside.

The caveats are real. It is one benchmark and one model family. The answer-only arm gets more calls, sixteen point seven per task against up to sixteen. On a second, stronger model the answer-only lead widened past thirty tasks, which cuts against the idea that the price shrinks with capability. Pretraining contamination of the public split is not excluded.

The governance read, mine again: this is a concrete price tag on verifiability as an oversight mechanism, and a small demonstration that a verifier which checks only the evidence the generator already saw can pass something wrong. It says little about adversarial settings.

Multi-value alignment by expert merging

Fifth, M A P E S, Multi-Value Alignment Pipeline via Expert Specialization, from Korea University. It is an engineering recipe for aligning a model to four values at once: instruction-following, honesty, helpfulness and truthfulness.

It has four stages. Preference pairs from Ultra Feedback are routed to the single value they best explain. A separate Lora adapter is trained per value. The adapters are merged with a new rule, called P A V M, that weights each expert per parameter by how well its change aligns with its own preference-improvement direction. Then a short harmonisation stage, value-conditioned preference training plus a distillation term, repairs the merged model.

On four instruction-tuned models from three to eight billion parameters, it has the best five-benchmark average in every case, beating standard D P O by three point three to six point seven points and the strongest competing baseline by one point seven to three point six. On Qwen two point five, seven billion, the average goes from forty-one point one eight for the base to forty-nine point oh two.

The paper's own controls undercut the framing. Merging without the repair stage collapses the model. Plain preference training in the repair stage already recovers to forty-six point three nine, and the distillation term adds about two point six more, so most of the recovery is ordinary post-merge training. The new merge rule adds about one point seven over the standard rule it generalises, forty-nine point oh two against forty-seven point two nine. An untrained uniform average of the same experts trails by only one point seven to four point two. And the conflict story is weaker than advertised: all six pairwise gradient cosines are positive, averaging zero point five one, so there is a common improvement direction.

The evidence is also thin for safety purposes. Honesty and helpfulness are scored by log-likelihood preference accuracy on small subsets, sixty-one and fifty-nine items. Five hundred and seventy-six training prompts appear verbatim in the truthfulness benchmark. Harmlessness was never trained, and its improvement is a correlation with the trained values, measured by the same kind of accuracy. The authors say the margin over the best baseline is smallest on the largest model, and they tested only models up to eight billion parameters. The governance read is modest. This is a capabilities-side recipe whose honesty gains should not be read as deployed honesty. The one thing worth noting is cost: under forty-five G P U hours on two consumer cards, so it is cheap for anyone to reproduce.

Realised versus population reliability

Sixth, Realized versus Population Reliability: Anytime-Valid Self-Consistency Certification under Correlated Reasoning. It is in the evaluations category, a statistics paper from Hunter College and Rutgers.

Self-consistency certificates sample a model repeatedly and stop when an anytime-valid test statistic crosses a threshold, with the false-certification rate controlled by a classical inequality. All of them assume the rollouts are conditionally independent. The paper's point is that when rollouts are positively correlated, because they share a reasoning prefix or because evidence is pooled across prompts of different difficulty, the null hypothesis splits into two. A realised null asks whether, for the reasoning actually drawn, the answer is favoured. A population null asks whether it is favoured on average over the model's randomness or the task. With independence they coincide. With correlation they come apart.

The standard betting certificate stays exactly valid for the realised claim, but read as a population claim it can fire far more often than its nominal level. And piling on rollouts for one prompt cannot fix it: the effective sample size is capped at about one over the correlation, so with a correlation near zero point four five, one prompt is worth about two independent rollouts. The remedy is to accumulate evidence across prompts, and the variance-minimising allocation under a pure rollout budget is one rollout per prompt.

On Claude Haiku four point five at temperature one, the measured correlation was zero point four three five on three hundred G S M eight K prompts and zero point four eight on one hundred and fifty A I M E prompts. On A I M E the single-prompt rule certified twenty-four percent of prompts at a nominal five percent level, even though the estimated average margin was negative, minus zero point one five. The prompt-level certificate fired on none.

The limits are the authors' own: one model, two benchmarks, a single seed, and the within-prompt shared-prefix correlation unmeasured. The twenty-four percent is a per-prompt crossing rate, not a type-one error estimate. For governance, the useful point is simple: a per-prompt agreement certificate is a claim about that prompt, not about the model, so per-prompt crossing rates should never be reported as a reliability rate. It also says nothing about whether agreement tracks correctness.

Evaluation awareness in single neurons

Seventh, Disentangling Verbalized Evaluation Awareness and Refusal through Neuron-Level Intervention, an interpretability paper from the University of Tokyo.

Reasoning models refuse more when their chain of thought says they are being tested. Earlier work steered a direction in the residual stream and saw refusal change, but a steering vector can overlap with refusal directly, so it cannot show that awareness causes the refusal. The authors work with individual M L P neurons instead. They label sentences that voice evaluation awareness, using a language-model judge, and rank neurons by the effect size separating those sentences from other reasoning. In QwQ thirty-two B, clamping the top five thousand such neurons to their ordinary mean drops the rate of verbalised awareness from between twelve point six and fifty-one point eight percent to at most one point four, while random neurons have little effect. Accuracy on three capability benchmarks stays within noise, although the check is coarse. A linear probe for unverbalised awareness also falls, but the probe is weak, with an A U C of zero point eight one four, and random neurons produce about fifty-four percent of its drop on prompts.

The central result is that these neurons also carry refusal. Refusal falls under the intervention, and on questions where no awareness text appears in either condition it still falls, from zero point five six five to zero point four six four at thirty thousand neurons. A decomposition on SorryBench attributes only four to ten percent of the drop to the missing awareness text, with intervals that include zero, and forty-eight to fifty-seven percent to the neurons writing directly along a refusal direction. Neurons strongly aligned with that direction are four to six and a half times more common among the awareness neurons than among random ones. A refusal-preserving version of the intervention partly restores refusal at similar awareness suppression.

A second result concerns training. Across the Olmo three checkpoints, the final reinforcement-learning stage has more awareness and more, and more selective, awareness neurons than the supervised and preference-tuning stages. The authors hedge this carefully: it is one trajectory, correlational, and the neurons are not shown to be the route.

The limits matter. The refusal decomposition rests on one model, four hundred and forty questions and three seeds. Judges are language models. Clamping up to thirty thousand of about one point eight million neurons is blunt, and it needs white-box access. The governance read, partly mine: evaluation awareness is not a clean nuisance variable you can subtract from safety scores, because part of measured refusal is entangled with the circuitry that notices testing. And if later training stages raise awareness, their safety results deserve extra scrutiny. Nothing here shows deployment behaviour directly.

Memory design for speculative decoding

Eighth, FOREST, a proposed hardware architecture for speculative decoding, in the compute and hardware category. Authors are from a chip company and Chinese universities and institutes.

In speculative decoding a small draft model proposes tokens and a large model verifies them in one pass. The authors say the three jobs, verification, drafting and attention over growing key-value caches, want different memory, yet a G P U serves all three from one high-bandwidth memory system. FOREST gives each its own tree. High-bandwidth flash holds the large model's weights, two stacks of three-dimensional resistive memory hold the draft model, and conventional high-bandwidth memory holds the caches and attention, each topped by a small digital compute-in-memory plate.

It is a simulation, not silicon. Traces recorded on real A eight hundred G P Us are replayed through a model that uses synthesis at twenty-two nanometres and assumed parameters for flash and resistive memory, which are emerging parts. Across six target and draft pairings from thirty-two to four hundred and five billion parameters, it claims up to five point five times end-to-end speedup, eleven point four six times decode throughput, sixteen point five times decode energy efficiency, and up to six point seven three times more tokens per dollar. The one clear loss is Llama three point one, four hundred and five billion, where compute-heavy prefill makes the G P U one point three two times faster at four thousand and ninety-six output tokens.

Treat the multipliers as upper bounds. The baseline is an older, export-compliant A eight hundred rather than a current datacentre part. Everything runs at batch size one with greedy decoding, which flatters latency-oriented designs and says little about batched serving. The cost result rests on an assumed price of twenty-two thousand dollars for a package no one sells yet.

The governance read is mine. It points toward inference hardware not defined by G P U compute and high-bandwidth memory alone, where capacity from flash and small custom compute might change what counts as a chokepoint for running very large models. The paper itself makes no policy claims, and the attention tree still relies on the newest high-bandwidth memory.

Strategy logic for adversarial agents

Ninth, S L I C R, Strategy Logic-Guided Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning, from Michigan State University. It is a capabilities paper with little safety content.

The idea is to state the goal of an agent in an adversarial game as a Strategy Logic formula, such as: for every opponent strategy there exists a response of mine that reaches the objective. The method turns the temporal part of the formula into a numeric robustness reward, learns a latent embedding of the opponents' strategy from the agent's own local history, and uses a shield, a classifier that throws out experience from opponents that just act randomly.

It is tested on Predator-Prey, three football scenarios, and a home-built extension of the StarCraft benchmark with populations of opponent teams. Against the opponent-modelling baseline L I A M, the headline is an improvement of up to ninety percentage points of win rate, on the hardest StarCraft map in the unseen setting, where the baseline rarely wins. On the seen setting of that map it reaches one hundred percent against about forty. On the easier maps the gains are smaller, ten to thirty points.

Read it with care. The ninety is a best case against a single baseline, with no significance tests. Unseen opponents come from the same or neighbouring algorithm families. The shield recognises only uniformly random opponents, so deceptive or mid-episode adaptive adversaries are not covered. The theory needs an information-preserving embedding, which the authors say may be hard to meet and do not test, and the formula's advantage over a shaped reward is mostly early-training speed. The governance relevance is low, apart from the idea of specification-guided training against adversaries that nothing here tests at frontier scale.

Hidden common causes

Last, The Most Likely Common Cause, a theory paper from physicists in Yerevan. It is not about A I.

Suppose two variables are correlated, a hidden common cause is known to exist, and you see only their joint distribution. The cause cannot be identified, because the mixture is non-identifiable. The authors use a generalised likelihood with a parameter just below one, which turns out to be equivalent to choosing the cause with the largest entropy given the observations: the least committal cause consistent with the data. For two symmetric binary variables the solution changes non-analytically at the switch from correlation to anti-correlation, like a second-order phase transition. For three variables the most likely cause can give structures that no time-ordered causal graph can represent. They argue this beats sparse alternatives such as minimum common entropy, which can set some probabilities to zero.

It is pure theory with small numerical examples. The cause's number of values is assumed known, probabilities are assumed known exactly, and there is no real data, no sample-size analysis and no scaling study. The governance relevance is only a general reminder: when a common cause is merely assumed, the choice of regulariser decides which cause you infer, so an inferred latent structure is a reference prior, not a finding.
