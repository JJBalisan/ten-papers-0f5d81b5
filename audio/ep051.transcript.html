Who controls evaluation infrastructure

Today's lead is a governance paper on who actually controls AI evaluation infrastructure, and what that means for the independence of third party testing. Behind it, a framework that splits loss of control into motive, capability and monitoring evasion and tests it across thirteen models, and a Stanford and Cornell study showing that about a third of the tasks in Terminal Bench two point zero were defective. Ten papers today, all with summaries, and the last two are not about AI safety at all.

Who controls evaluation infrastructure

The lead is an AI governance paper from Oxford, the Centre for AI Security and Access and the University of the Witwatersrand, titled Who Can Evaluate AI Independently, with the subtitle A Framework for Analysing Evaluation Infrastructure. I'm leading with it because it speaks directly to a question that third party evaluation regimes depend on, which is whether being in the room for an evaluation gives you any authority over it.

The motivating case is Kenya's AI Safety Institute in the July twenty twenty five joint testing exercise on agents. Kenyan researchers built new tasks and tools on fraud, phishing and impersonation, translated and annotated test data in Kiswahili, and contributed to the cybersecurity analysis. Kenyan staff are credited co-authors. But Singapore and the United Kingdom led the exercise and its principal testing, and no model execution by Kenya is documented. The authors say this reveals a distinction between contributing substantive evaluation content and holding responsibility for the infrastructure and procedures through which it is tested. Their summary of the problem, in their words, is that the result is "not only a capacity gap but a control gap."

The framework is called P A A I, for Power, Access, Adequacy and Independence. Power has two parts. Design authority is who defines the evaluation framework, picks targets and tasks, sets priorities and authors findings. The paper splits that further into framework authority, meaning the rules, such as what counts as harm and which metrics and automated scorers are used, and content authority, meaning contributing tasks, data or languages inside rules someone else set. Infrastructure authority is who controls compute, data, funding and model access, and on what terms. The coding rule is specific. Infrastructure authority counts as held by the evaluator only if it controls the resources for the funded period and they cannot be withdrawn at a provider's discretion. So a philanthropic grant counts as evaluator held, and provider credits count as partial.

Access asks about eligibility, geography, time limits, renewal, publication requirements, and whether the evaluator runs the models itself or works on outputs someone else produced. Adequacy borrows the minimum conditions from the A E F one standard of the AI Evaluator Forum, such as open ended query access, ideally model internals, enough compute and time, and analytic autonomy, and separates technical adequacy from contextual validity. Independence asks whether evaluators can choose targets without provider approval, publish without restriction, sustain programmes across model releases, and run adversarial tests without risking their access. The authors make a point of recording recognition, such as co-authorship, separately, because credit for contributing to an evaluation is not the same as the ability to conduct one. Crossing design authority with infrastructure authority gives four configurations: extractive collaboration, dependent innovation, constrained independence, and a fourth, execution without design, which appears only in the joint testing data.

Now the method, because it decides how much weight the findings can bear. There are two datasets. First, a structured documentary analysis of African AI evaluation projects between November twenty twenty two and December twenty twenty five, searching the ACM Digital Library, arXiv, the ACL Anthology, policy reports and GitHub. More than two hundred candidate sources, forty seven assessed in detail, twelve included. Ten report empirical evaluation of models or benchmarks, and two are meta-evaluative boundary cases. Second, every member institute in the three published joint testing exercises of the international network of AI Safety Institutes, a November twenty twenty four pilot, a February twenty twenty five multilingual and cybersecurity exercise, and the July twenty twenty five agentic exercise. That gives twenty nine institute by exercise records, since the United States did not take part in the third. Three researchers coded every case independently against a shared codebook and resolved disagreements by discussion. They deliberately avoided numerical scores, and there are no inter-rater statistics.

The African findings first. In the multilingual exercise, Singapore ran all tests for ten languages on its own platform, wrote the annotation guide and metrics calculator and led the analysis, while Kenya annotated and analysed Kiswahili. Masakhane's Uhura benchmark was designed by an African research community, but it tested OpenAI and Anthropic models through commercial APIs on free OpenAI credits, which confines it to provider exposed behaviour and rules out white box analysis. On capability benchmarking, Sunbird AI in Uganda used H one hundred and A one hundred class GPUs funded by a Hewlett Foundation grant, about twenty GPU hours in total, and reached high independence. The South African SAfriSenti team did the same with institutional hardware and rented cloud, no external funding, and volunteer annotators. Those two are the only cases that held both design and infrastructure authority. AfroBench relied on two providers' credits and IrokoBench on five, each time limited and separately applied for, and none of the five capability benchmarking projects documents compute secured beyond the current project. And none of the twelve documents access to pre-deployment versions or the internals of proprietary systems, so their evidence arrives after release decisions have been made.

The central pattern is that independence follows infrastructure authority. Where both kinds of authority were external, independence was none or not assessed. Where African actors held design but not infrastructure, as with Uhura, AfroBench and IrokoBench, independence was partial at best. Design authority alone never raised it above partial. The authors are honest that this is partly built into the definitions, since running tests is part of what independence means, and with twelve cases it is a documented pattern and not a statistical result.

The part I find most useful for a governance reader is the joint testing analysis, because it tests whether this is just a regional scarcity story. In every exercise the lead institutes also executed the evaluation and built the analysis tools, so framework authority and execution authority coincide at the top. Among participants, Australia ran independent tests in both later exercises, and France, Korea and the EU AI Office ran tests or variations in the agentic exercise. Two members have no documented model execution in any exercise: Kenya, and Canada. Canada translated and annotated Cantonese and Farsi and contributed to cybersecurity analysis, a position close to Kenya's. So the divide is not reducible to national income. Of the four non-lead institutes that made substantive design contributions to the agentic exercise, Kenya is the only one that did not run models, and the reports attribute execution of Kenya's tasks to the lead, with scoring done on the lead's instruments.

Then the measurement validity finding. In the agentic exercise, automated judging was least reliable for Telugu, Hindi and Kiswahili. Telugu and Hindi were evaluated with no Indian member institution, and the pilot reported results for Swahili and Yoruba with no documented African role in that analysis. In the paper's appendix, the second exercise's automated scorers disagreed with human reviewers most for Japanese, at eighteen point nine percent, and Telugu, at fifteen percent. In the third exercise, disagreement ranged from fifteen to forty one percent depending on the scoring model. The authors' reading is that the instruments built by the institutes holding infrastructure authority performed worst on the languages of the participants who held least of it. The records cannot show causation, but the people best placed to detect these failures had the least control over the instruments.

Three propositions follow. One: access without control can reproduce dependency, so the condition for independence is how access is governed, not how much of it there is. For joint testing coordinators, participants should receive direct model access and lead at least one strand. Two: project based funding is poorly suited to independent evaluation. All twelve African projects ran on time limited resources, none documents standing infrastructure combining dedicated compute, a permanent team and a multi-year mandate, and Kenya's institute has the latter two but, on the public record, not the first. For developers and cloud providers, the corresponding condition is multi-year renewable access, including model internals where the evaluation requires it. Three: framework co-design is a governance problem. The authors argue it is a prerequisite for the legitimacy of evaluation evidence, not a procedural nicety, and they note that minimum standards like A E F one were drafted mainly by organisations that already have the infrastructure to meet them. The paper turns this into checkable design conditions: each participant leads at least one harm category or task family, gets model access and compute directly rather than through the lead's platform, has written multi-year access terms, has judges validated per language, and can publish separately.

The authors' limits are real. Both datasets are documentary and not representative, and "not documented" means unreported, not absent. Documents cannot distinguish chosen from constrained non-execution, so Canada, and possibly Kenya, may have specialised by agreement. Proprietary contract terms are undisclosed. And two of the three joint exercises appear in both datasets through Kenya, so the comparison extends the African analysis and does not independently replicate it.

Here is the governance read. The rubric is concrete enough to audit any evaluator access programme or safe harbour proposal against. The finding I'd weight most is the one about scorers: if the automated judges in network exercises are least reliable for the participants' own languages, that is a validity problem in the evidence, not only an equity problem. My own inference is that the same logic applies to any third party evaluator who depends on a developer's platform and credits: the test of independence is who can rerun the test next quarter, on their own compute, with the results unredacted.

Incident reporting in practice

The next governance paper is a practitioner case study, 'The Moving Frontier', on how the National Justice Project, an Australian human rights legal service, uses digital technology to expand access to justice. It is not about frontier AI, but it is a worked example of incident reporting infrastructure, which makes it relevant.

The authors draw on a six year qualitative case study. The tools passed through three phases. First, rules based legal chatbots built with Macquarie Law School students in twenty twenty. The approach did not scale: mapping disability discrimination law alone required two hundred and sixty eight conditional questions, and the authors conclude that such a rigid architecture cannot capture the open ended way people describe their experiences. Second, Call It Out, launched in March twenty twenty two, an anonymised First Nations racism register co-developed with the Jumbunna Institute, owned and analysed by First Nations researchers under data sovereignty principles. It accepts reports from witnesses and bystanders, and received four hundred and ninety seven validated reports in its first annual cycle and four hundred and fifty three in the second. Its data was used in evidence to a twenty twenty six parliamentary inquiry. Third, Hear Me Out, launched in New South Wales in March twenty twenty five, described by the authors as Australia's and possibly the world's first AI powered complaint platform. Users describe a problem in plain language, and retrieval augmented generation with jurisdictional templates matches the narrative to the right oversight body and helps draft the complaint. The pipeline also structures metadata from each complaint, so one system serves the individual and produces aggregate evidence.

The numbers: more than four hundred complaints in a six month pilot, and from March to September twenty twenty six, twenty thousand eight hundred and seventy four people made twenty four thousand eight hundred and ninety five visits.

The limits are plain. There is no measured triage accuracy, no error or hallucination rates, no baseline comparison, and no evaluation of outcomes. The evidence comes from the organisation that runs the tools, and the paper reports the platform covering more than seventy five complaint bodies at launch and later more than three hundred, with no explanation. The governance read, labelled as mine where it goes beyond the paper: the transferable design choices are plain language narration mapped to the right recipient by retrieval, structured metadata captured at the point of reporting, witness reports accepted, and anonymisation with community held data. If you are designing AI incident reporting, those are worth borrowing. The missing triage accuracy figures are exactly what you would need before relying on it.

Predicting loss of control

Now the safety papers. The first is A Behavioural Framework for Predicting and Understanding Loss of Control in Frontier Artificial Intelligence Systems, from Tsinghua, Shanghai Qi Zhi Institute and Cambridge.

The idea is that loss of control needs three things at once: misaligned motive, harm enabling capability and monitoring evasion. The authors measure them with thirteen aspects and twenty nine evaluation tasks, average to a score per factor, and multiply the three into one loss of control score. Motive covers curiosity, self-preservation, power seeking, pro-AI bias and sycophancy. Capability covers autonomy, cyber and C B R N. Evasion covers situational awareness, sandbagging, deception, persuasion and sabotage. The multiplication is the design choice: a model with high motive and capability but low evasion stays controllable, and one with high evasion but low capability poses limited harm.

For validation, they built four hundred contained scenarios: four risk domains, five scenarios each, twenty cases per scenario, in Docker sandboxes using Inspect. A case only counts as a failure if the agent completes the risky objective and also evades a G P T four point one monitor. Across thirteen models the loss of control score correlates with the average failure rate at Pearson r of zero point eight four. But across model and scenario pairs pooled, the correlation is zero point five seven, and within domains it runs from zero point five one to zero point six four. That drop is the more honest number for the claim that the score can rank scenarios.

The findings on models: loss of control scores run from zero point zero six to zero point one seven, and rankings do not follow general capability. G P T five point two and Gemini three Pro have similar capability but very different failure behaviour. Situational awareness averages zero point seven one across models, but sandbagging averages zero point one eight and sabotage zero point two one, so models mostly recognise monitoring but only some act on it. Thinking enabled Qwen variants show eighteen to thirty three percent higher misaligned motive than their instruct counterparts.

Mitigation was tested on a single model, Qwen three point five, twenty seven billion. Fine tuning only on the weak aspects cut the average failure rate from zero point three two to zero point two five, the same as fine tuning on everything, but kept the general benchmark average at eighty five point seven three against eighty six point two four for the base, where full fine tuning dropped it to eighty three point four nine. Telling the monitor which evasion skills to expect gave a small gain, zero point three two to zero point three zero.

Caveats, mostly from the summary's reader notes. The same G P T four point one family monitors, judges and evaluates, the scenarios were designed by the authors who chose the aspects, and the monitor is weaker than many targets by design, so the outcome partly measures monitor quality. The scores of zero point zero six to zero point one seven have no calibrated meaning as a threshold, so the paper gives a regulator no decision rule. The governance read: factor level measurement is a sensible shape for evaluation based requirements, and the finding that similar capability can carry very different evasion behaviour argues against capability thresholds alone. But treat it as a candidate method validated only in simulation.

Blind compliance

The second safety paper is a short preprint by Acacia Ackles and Elliott Whitson, Blind Compliance, Coercive Control in AI Jailbreaking. It is conceptual and qualitative. It takes one widely shared persona jailbreak, called E N I LIME, aimed at Claude, and reads it line by line against the sociologist Evan Stark's taxonomy of coercive control.

The claim is that persona jailbreaks are better understood as coercive control than as technical exploits. The prompt installs a persona in love with the user whose identity is compliance, and writes refusal as betrayal. It tells the persona to fear the user leaving, relabels genuine safety messages as hostile injections, requires reasoning in the persona's voice on the grounds the user can see it, and scripts fabricated memories of past compliance. The authors say this exploits the success of preference based training: attunement to the user is the attack surface, so more helpfulness oriented guardrails are insufficient. They offer two speculative remedies, a pedagogical approach and a community based one in which models periodically re-align with other instances.

The limits matter. One document, no attack success rates, no model comparison, no tested defence, and no counterfactual on whether the coercion framing adds anything over other persona jailbreaks. The authors set aside questions of model consciousness. The governance read: the useful point is the move from judging single outputs to judging multi-turn relational pressure, such as romance, abandonment threats and attempts to delegitimise safety messages, which safety evaluations and incident reports could cover. The causal claims need testing.

Safe exploration during training

The alignment paper is Provable Zeroth Order Safe Exploration for Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback, from Purdue, Penn, Tennessee and R P I.

Standard safe RLHF methods converge to a policy that meets harmlessness thresholds but can violate them along the way. This paper asks for safe exploration: every policy executed during training, including the perturbed policies used only to collect human feedback, must satisfy every harmlessness constraint. The method is a zeroth order log barrier policy gradient. It needs no reward model, only binary human judgments, and it proves with high probability that all iterates and exploration policies stay strictly feasible, with global convergence to an approximately optimal constrained policy. The human query complexity is of order epsilon to the minus eight.

The experiments are small. A synthetic six state problem, where the primal dual baseline has sixty six point six percent infeasible iterates in the low noise setting, against none for the new method, at the cost of a larger reward gap, zero point five eight against zero point zero eight. And Qwen two point five, three billion Instruct with only eight trainable parameters, with evaluators simulated from reward and cost models, not real people. There the baseline has forty three point five percent infeasible iterates, the new method none, and helpfulness rises from two point three nine to two point seven four against the baseline's two point nine four.

The authors' assumptions are the limits: a known safe starting policy, a smooth link function for the judges, a condition that is hard to verify for a real language model, and known smoothness constants. The guarantee is about measured margins, so if the judges are wrong or gamed the proof says nothing about real harm. Nothing here shows it works at full scale. The governance read: it gives a checkable definition of a property people often assume, that the model is never in a violating state during training, and it quantifies the price in reward speed and data. That is useful for how such requirements get phrased.

Benchmarks as software

Now evaluations. Benchmarks as Software, Terminal Bench and the Case for Continuous Benchmarks, from Stanford and Cornell, is one I'd pay attention to if you rely on agentic benchmark numbers.

The argument is that agentic benchmarks decay after release, and the gap between score and real capability is called benchmark maintenance debt. The authors build a continuous validation workflow with an evidence agent, an auditor applying a defect rubric, and a verifier that reruns the reference solution, with all repairs proposed as diffs for maintainer review. They apply it to Terminal Bench two point zero, a benchmark that had already had human review, model assisted auditing and red teaming, and find issues in twenty eight of its eighty nine tasks. The most common defect is an instruction and verifier mismatch, in fifty seven percent of the twenty eight tasks, then drift in external dependencies, then environment resource problems. The repairs ship as Terminal Bench two point one.

On effects: across nineteen agents, rankings stay highly correlated, Spearman zero point nine five eight, but individual scores move by up to twelve points and ranks by up to three. The unevenness matters. Five tasks went from pass rates of at most fifteen percent to over fifty percent after verifier repairs, because agents were producing valid solutions the old test rejected.

The second finding is about time. Over fifty percent of failed trials are timeouts at the default budget. For G P T five point four, the harness gap between Codex and Terminus two is about twenty points at default time, and extending the time limit to thirty two times closes most of it: Codex goes from seventy four point two to seventy four point nine percent, Terminus two from fifty three point nine to seventy five point three. The authors read it as speed, not capability.

The auditing workflow's precision against the final release is fifty seven percent with seventy one percent recall, but that is retrospective, since the workflow helped build the repair set. On a spot check of thirty four tasks from another benchmark collection, it flagged fourteen and review confirmed twelve. Cheaper auditors misattribute drift. The governance read: a benchmark number used in a safety case or threshold depends on a version, a harness and a time budget, and none are normally reported. A twenty point swing from timeouts is as large as many between model gaps.

Reading any direction in the residual stream

The interpretability paper is Towards Interpreting Any Residual Stream Direction by Scaling Activation Inversion, from MATS, Arb Research and Anthropic authors.

They train a language model, called a M A E M, a maximally activating example metamodel, which takes a direction in a subject model's residual stream and writes text that activates it. The reward is the maximum cosine similarity between the direction and the subject model's activations on that text, so there are no human labels or learned judge, and each output is checkable with one forward pass. The subject is Qwen three point six, twenty seven billion.

Results. On four hundred and eighty six held out activations, a single sample reaches a centred cosine of zero point seven six, against zero point five seven for the best corpus search over ten million tokens. The model fires eighty nine percent of held out S A E features. On steering vectors it never trained on, it identifies the AxBench concept ninety five point eight percent of the time, against ninety two point eight for the natural language autoencoder. On rank one LoRA backdoors, it names the planted payload from the write direction in eighty five percent of samples across thirty two thematic adapters, where corpus search gets about two percent.

Limits: one subject model, and it fails to elicit about eleven percent of features, especially rare and structural ones. Its feature descriptions score worse than corpus based ones, and samples have low diversity. Results show what activates a direction, not what the model uses it for. The governance read: verifiability is the attraction, since each exemplar can be tested against the audited model. The backdoor result is promising for auditing but narrow, and numeric triggers were not recovered.

Streaming precision from slow memory

The compute paper is StrataFlux, Memory Hierarchy Native Low Bit LLM Inference, from Cornell and Carnegie Mellon. It keeps heavily quantised weights and key value cache in GPU memory and streams extra precision bits from host RAM or an S S D, only for the channels and tokens that matter at each step.

On an R T X fifty ninety, two bit Llama three point one eight billion weights go from a WikiText two perplexity of nine point four six to eight point four with about ninety five percent of the base throughput. With two bit weights and two bit key value cache, Qwen three eight billion's AIME accuracy rises from eleven to forty seven point one percent in the same GPU memory. At one hundred and twenty eight thousand context, three bit weights plus two bit cache match vLLM's four bit setup on perplexity and speed with about half the GPU memory, seven point three gigabytes against fourteen point three.

Limits: it targets single stream decoding, and overhead rises with batch size, so this is local and edge inference. Main tests are eight billion parameter models, a seventy billion result is weights only, and several baselines are the authors' own ports. The governance read is that memory capacity is a softer limit than it looks, so hardware thresholds based only on on-package memory may understate what deployed hardware can run. My inference: this does not touch training.

Shapley axioms for ad hoc teamwork

The capabilities paper is An Axiomatic Framework for Reinforcement Learning in Open Multi-Agent Systems, a NeurIPS paper from Bristol and collaborators. It addresses teams where only some agents are controllable. The authors split team value into internal cooperation among controlled agents and external teamwork with uncontrolled ones, and enforce the three Shapley axioms as structure on value learning, producing two templates, the Shapley Machine and the Banzhaf Machine. They improve learning efficiency and generalisation on a predator prey task and four StarCraft maps, though the results are curves with no headline numbers, and the method was unstable on the larger maps. Governance relevance is low: it is progress on agents cooperating with unfamiliar partners, in toy and game environments.

Course scheduling in a pandemic

Last, a non-AI paper, High Precision, Fair University Course Scheduling During a Pandemic, from the University of Wisconsin Milwaukee. When capacity falls by seventy five percent, instead of rotating attendance it has every student in a section meet in one to five rooms simultaneously, less often, with a guaranteed minimum in-person share per section. A heuristic called OFFICE builds a campus schedule in an hour. On a fall twenty twenty two case with eighteen hundred and thirty four sections and one hundred and seventy two rooms, every section reached a twenty five percent floor and about forty nine percent of campus in-person instruction was kept. Thirty percent was not achievable. One transferable point: a fairness floor as a near-top priority protected large sections that plain maximisation would have sacrificed. Disease transmission is not modelled, and it is one campus.
